> [!info] Using AI well is a more useful critique than cataloguing its failures
> Demonstrate thoughtful use in your own practice, and ground your concerns in what you've actually encountered. Critique that comes from using the tools carries more weight than a list of what they get wrong. The question worth asking is what you choose to amplify.

If your LinkedIn feed is anything like mine, you're seeing a steady stream of posts warning about the dangers of AI. They include critiques of tech companies'—and their CEOs'—motives, videos with errors presented as evidence of failure, and moral panic about the dangers of misinformation.

> Walk into any number of discussions about emerging technology and you'll find a predictable rhythm. New development announced. Immediate deconstruction follows. Corporate motives questioned. Potential harms catalogued. Limitations highlighted. Discussion concludes with knowing nods about capitalism, surveillance or inequality. *Carlo Iacono (2025), [Wonder as resistance](https://hybridhorizons.substack.com/p/wonder-as-resistance)*

This kind of engagement might be fair, but it isn't very helpful. There's a better game available for critiquing AI in education, and it starts with using the technology in your own practice.

## An alternative game
Technology amplifies human intention, though never neutrally. This is true of electricity, cars, and social media, and it's true of AI. Misinformation and cheating existed before [[large language models]], but so did education, creativity, and expertise. AI does bring problems of its own—[[Posts/2026-03-21-not-all-ai-errors-are-hallucinations|confident errors]], the biases in its training data, the commercial interests of the companies that build it—and those deserve attention. But much of what it does is amplify what we can already do, in whichever direction we choose to point it.

At the moment it feels like we're playing a game where every post tries to score points for one of two teams, 'AI is Good' or 'AI is Bad'. An alternative version of the game looks like this:

**Engage in good faith**. Use the tools to solve real problems in your work rather than generating examples designed to fail. Prompt thoughtfully, choose appropriate applications, and learn what works and what doesn't through practice.

**Look for value**. When a new capability appears, ask what it makes possible, what would make it more useful, and what's almost within reach if the technology improves.

**Demonstrate use**. Show how you actually work with AI: what works, what doesn't, and what you learned. Making your practice visible lets others learn from it.

**Critique from practice**. Ground your concerns in actual use. Point to specific problems you've encountered and suggest alternatives based on what you've tried, which is far more useful than an imagined worst case.

**Expose systemic problems**. Use AI to reveal where existing systems fall short. Much of our assessment doesn't measure what it claims to. If a language model can write a passable reflective account of a birth a student midwife supported, the assessment was always easier to satisfy with fluent writing about reflection than with reflection itself. Publishing incentives in research are misaligned in similar ways, and the gatekeeping and access problems in both existed long before AI. The technology can expose these problems, and you can use that exposure productively.

## What thoughtful AI use looks like
I use AI daily: to brainstorm and get feedback on my thinking, for quick literature reviews and synthesising documentation, to peer-review material before I share it with others, to support my learning when I'm trying to understand something complex, and as an editor for my writing.

I teach my students to use it the same way, as a thinking partner that supports their learning and research and helps them engage with ideas more effectively. They *can* use it to generate outputs they submit as their own work, and we talk about why [[learning-alignment|this misses the point]]. They're in that classroom to develop their capacity to think critically and learn independently, and where AI helps with that, I work with them to explore what it looks like in practice.

These are small, daily practices that make my work more productive and (I hope) my students' learning more effective, and anyone can adopt them. What stands in the way is orientation more than technical skill: are you looking for what might go wrong, or what might work?

It's like the difference between reading a student's work for evidence of cheating and reading it for evidence of learning. The data is the same, but one lens assumes the worst of people and the other looks for growth and capability. Research on confirmation bias shows how strong the pull is: we find the evidence we look for (Nickerson, 1998), and assessment is no different.

## The choice
To be fair, the critical posts I see on social media could serve a purpose. They raise concerns, point out limitations, and question motives. But if that's all they do, we're spending our time and energy imagining problems instead of demonstrating solutions, and missing the chance to shape how this technology develops. We're also choosing to amplify a negative view of the world.

You could write another post about a failure you've seen someone else share. The failures are real, and the ones you run into in your own work are worth writing about. But you could also use the technology to make your research more accessible, to help students develop critical thinking, to surface connections across literatures, to make conferences more inclusive, and to expose broken systems and suggest alternatives.

One of these maintains the status quo while performing concern from the sidelines, and the other works towards something better.

Next time you see a post describing AI's failures or questioning tech companies' motives, remember that there's another game available.

The question then becomes: what will you choose to amplify?
