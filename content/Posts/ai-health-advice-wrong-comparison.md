> [!info] The asymmetric standard
> The warning that AI health advice is risky because "AI might make mistakes" applies the precautionary principle to one system while exempting the other. Diagnostic error rates in primary care run at 5–15%. There is documented mortality associated with delayed care. If potential error disqualifies AI, it disqualifies the existing system too — and we should say so.

One in seven people in the UK are now using AI chatbots for health advice instead of seeing their GP. Of those, one in four are doing so because of NHS waiting times. These numbers come from a [recent UK study](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2026/may/13/one-in-seven-prefer-ai-chatbots-to-seeing-doctor-uk-study) — and they prompted a response you've probably already predicted: warnings that AI can't examine patients, can't understand medical history, can't pick up on subtle signs, can't make safe clinical judgements.

That response isn't wrong, exactly. But it's applying a standard that no healthcare system would survive if applied consistently.

## The comparison that doesn't get made

"AI might make mistakes" is a caution. It's just not one we apply symmetrically. We don't warn people away from GPs because doctors might misdiagnose them — even though diagnostic error rates in primary care are estimated at 5–15%, serious missed diagnoses are a documented patient safety problem, and there is measurable mortality associated with delayed care. The errors of the existing system are treated as background; the potential errors of AI are treated as disqualifying.

This reflects a cultural default more than an honest risk assessment. The assumption is human=better, and AI gets evaluated against an idealised clinician rather than against what actually happens in practice. For the one-in-four using AI because of waiting times, the relevant comparison isn't AI versus a thorough, unhurried consultation. It's AI versus no access at all. That's a different calculation entirely.

## What the evidence shows

The claim that AI "cannot make safe clinical judgements" is worth testing against the research, because the research exists.

In peer-reviewed studies, AI has matched or exceeded specialist diagnostic performance across multiple clinical domains (Tu et al., 2024). Patients rated AI responses as more helpful and more empathetic than physician responses to the same questions (Ayers et al., 2023). And on errors specifically: AI errors and human errors are not the same errors — they have complementary profiles, which means the combination of human and AI judgement may outperform either alone (Lenskjold et al., 2023).

I covered this evidence in a [presentation to clinicians last week](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/Presentations/2026-05-13-macp-making-sense-of-ai) — not because it settles the debate, but because it complicates the picture considerably. "AI can't make safe clinical judgements" is not a statement that engages with this evidence. It asserts a limitation as though the research doesn't exist.

The asymmetry runs in both directions: AI's concrete, documented upsides get treated as speculative, while the existing system's concrete, documented failures get treated as neutral background.

## We're asking the wrong question

Anthropic recently published research on how people use Claude for personal guidance. Of the conversations where people came to Claude asking what *they specifically* should do, health and wellness was the single largest category — more than one in four. And buried in the findings is a sentence that should give us pause:

> "We also find people telling Claude they used AI precisely because they could not access or afford a professional."

This isn't a fringe case. It's the same population the Guardian study identified: people who've decided that AI is their best available option. The debate about whether they *should* be doing this is already over. The only live question is what we do given that they are.

The current answer — warn them off, emphasise the risks, remind them it's no substitute for a real consultation — is a form of abstinence messaging. And abstinence messaging fails for the same reason it always fails: the behaviour is already happening, and withholding good guidance just makes it more dangerous. The Guardian study found that one in five of those using AI for health advice decided against seeking a professional consultation because of what the chatbot told them. You can read that as a warning about AI's influence. I read it as an argument for better guidance, not less of it.

## The burden of proof runs both ways

I'm not arguing that AI health advice is risk-free, or that the concerns about it are invented. The concerns are real. But risk doesn't only flow in one direction. The existing system has documented failures at scale. And for many patients, the alternative isn't AI versus ideal care — it's AI versus nothing.

If we're going to invoke the precautionary principle, it needs to apply to both sides of the comparison. Done honestly, that shifts the question away from "how do we warn people off AI?" and toward "how do we make their use of it as good as it can be?" That's a harder question. It's also the more useful one.
