> [!info] When creation is trivial, evaluation becomes the work
> AI can learn your patterns—what you typically choose. But it can't judge whether those patterns produce outcomes worth amplifying. As creation and curation become trivial, judgement about what *should* exist becomes the primary human contribution.

Content creation has become trivially easy, and what used to take hours now takes minutes. The technical barrier to producing text, images, presentations, and video has collapsed in ways that seemed impossible just two years ago.

Unfortunately, the result is slop: low-effort output flooding every channel. The common response is to valorise curation—we tell ourselves that creation is cheap but curation is expensive, that the real skill is knowing what to make, not how to make it.

But this framing misses something more fundamental: [[taste-and-judgement|taste]], or judgement about what should exist.

## Creation, curation, and taste

Three different things are getting conflated:

- **Creation**: the technical execution—writing, building, coordinating—which is becoming trivially easy.
- **Curation**: the process of selecting and organising—choosing what to make from available options. This is also becoming easier as AI learns your patterns and preferences.
- **Taste**: judgement about what matters and what should exist. This is fundamentally different from the other two.

The distinction matters because AI can be descriptive but not evaluative. It can learn what you typically choose—your patterns, preferences, and historical decisions—but learning patterns is not the same as evaluating whether they're worth perpetuating. You might have consistent preferences like writing style and reading lists (which AI can learn), but questionable taste (which it can't replace).

Taste has a well-studied relative. Assessment researchers call it evaluative judgement: the capability to judge the quality of your own work and the work of others (Tai et al., 2018). [[Bibliography/Bearman-et-al-2024-developing-evaluative-judgement-for-a-time-of-generative-artificial-intelligence|Bearman et al. (2024)]] argue that generative AI makes developing it urgent, because students now receive fluent output they didn't make and have to decide whether it's any good, and they conclude that we shouldn't give up our role as arbiters of quality.

Taste, as I'm using the word here, builds on that capability and stretches it. Evaluative judgement asks whether something is good; taste also asks whether it should exist at all, and you can't answer the second question well without being able to answer the first.

You might object: a well-aligned[^ai-alignment] AI could help improve taste by questioning your choices. We may reach a point where AI challenges your decisions, suggests you're optimising for the wrong outcomes, or refuses to generate obvious slop. But this raises uncomfortable questions about who decides what constitutes 'good taste' or 'slop'—you, the AI, or the companies building these systems? These are important considerations, but I don't think they change the core point: even if AI challenges your choices, you're still the one making the final evaluation.

What remains is the harder question: do your patterns—the things you consistently choose to do—reflect good judgement about what should exist in the world?

## From execution to evaluation

Professional value has traditionally been bound to execution: the ability to write compelling arguments, design effective systems, or analyse complex data. Professional identity grows out of these craft skills too.

This relationship is breaking down. The craft itself is increasingly automated—[[large language models|generative AI]] can now read and write on my behalf, drawing on my historical patterns and preferences. What can't be automated is the judgement that determines what *should* be done in the first place.

A small example: writing a blog post. Previously, skill meant wordsmithing—finding the right phrase, structuring arguments, maintaining voice. Now, skill means evaluating whether this particular idea deserves to exist in this particular form for this particular audience at this particular moment.

A bigger example: coordinating [[Notes/ai-agents|AI agents]] to manage a school. Technical orchestration matters, but value lives in the taste shaping every interaction—what counts as good tutoring, when to escalate questions to humans, how to balance efficiency with empathy. This judgement cascades through thousands of interactions daily.

Taste, unlike technical skill or learned preferences, scales in unexpected ways when mediated by AI.

## Why taste matters at scale

Someone with poor taste writing blog posts creates localised slop—annoying but containable. That same person coordinating AI systems creates scaled harm. Poor taste amplified produces outcomes nobody wants: manipulative interfaces, metrics optimised for irrelevance, decisions that serve systems rather than people.

The inverse is also true. Good taste amplified creates disproportionate value. Someone with refined judgement about what matters can coordinate systems that produce better outcomes than they could ever create individually.

So the problem at scale is one of taste, and taste doesn't improve on its own through practice. Someone has to cultivate it deliberately.

## What cultivation looks like

Taste develops through exposure and reflection. You encounter diverse examples, good and bad, and work out why particular approaches or outcomes work or fail. The evaluative judgement research shows how to do this deliberately rather than leaving it to experience: self-assessment, peer review, working with exemplars, and feedback on the judgements themselves, not only on the work (Tai et al., 2018). Taste operates across interconnected dimensions:

**Aesthetic taste**: What creates clarity versus obscurity? When does complexity serve understanding?

**Strategic taste**: What problems actually matter? What creates lasting value versus temporary fixes?

**Ethical taste**: Who benefits and who bears costs? What are the second-order effects?

The three overlap, and together they make up a single sensibility about what's worth doing.

Cultivating taste involves:

- Paying attention to why some approaches succeed where others fail
- Finding examples of excellence and examining what makes them excellent
- Developing sensitivity to when something feels wrong, even if you can't articulate why
- Building confidence to trust your judgement while staying open to being wrong
- Exposing yourself to perspectives that challenge your default assumptions

In clinical education, this can be built into ordinary supervision. A speech and language therapy student uses AI to draft three discharge letters for a patient they've treated, ranks them, and defends the ranking to their supervisor, who has ranked them independently. Where the rankings differ, the standard the supervisor has been applying without saying so becomes something the two of them can discuss. That is evaluative judgement: is this letter good? The taste question follows: what does this patient's GP actually need to know, and would three lines serve them better than two pages?

Skill development follows clearer progression paths. Taste depends on evaluative judgement, which means you're sometimes going to be wrong, and it asks you to care enough to form strong opinions while staying open to revising them.

## The identity shift

If you've built your professional identity around execution (writing well, analysing competently, designing effectively), this shift is going to be uncomfortable. These capabilities still matter, but they're no longer sufficient. The question shifts from "can I do this well?" to "should this be done at all?"

You can see this playing out in universities right now. Institutions are focused on detecting AI use in student assessments, worried about whether students are doing their own writing, when the more important question is whether we should be assessing through essays at all. Staff are using AI to read and write reports, speeding up the process, when they should be asking whether those reports need to exist in the first place. We're optimising execution when we should be evaluating purpose.

More and more, what you shape is a system that produces artefacts, decisions, and actions at scale, and the main constraint on that system is your taste: your judgement about what deserves to exist.

## When taste is finally enough

This shift isn't purely threatening. For most of human history, having good taste wasn't enough. You might know exactly what should exist without having the technical ability to make it real. The gap between vision and execution was enormous.

But if you can discern what matters and what deserves to be made real, you can increasingly coordinate systems that give form to ideas.

This makes taste accessible as a primary form of value, though craft still has a part to play. Evaluative judgement is built by making things and holding them against a standard, and the student in the example above can judge those letters because they've written letters of their own and seen what happens to them. What changes is how much craft you need before you can contribute. You need enough to know good work when you see it, and the discernment to decide what's worth making, and that takes a good deal less than the decade of mastery it once did.

And taste is contagious. When you encounter something made with care and judgement, it shifts your sense of what's possible. Good taste creates standards that elevate surrounding work. As more people develop taste and coordinate AI systems to express it, we can create environments where excellence becomes more visible and reproducible.

## An invitation

So: what are you putting into the world? I mean the outcomes as well as the content. What happens because you chose to create this rather than something else?

If you're satisfied with your answer, and your taste produces outcomes you'd want amplified, then the work going forward is refinement: making it more precise, more generous, and more attuned to what matters.

If you're not satisfied, because you recognise that your use of AI is adding noise rather than signal, then your work is cultivation, and you get to decide what deserves to exist.

The technical barriers have collapsed. What remains is a deeply human question: what's worth making? What should exist that doesn't yet exist? What outcome would make the world incrementally better?

These are questions of taste, and taste isn't zero-sum. My developing better taste doesn't prevent yours. It potentially helps, because exposure to excellent work improves everyone's discernment.

This is the opportunity hidden in the slop crisis: to become people whose taste shapes better outcomes, who understand what matters and can coordinate systems to realise it, and who add signal rather than noise.

That's what becomes possible when both creation and curation cost nothing, and evaluation becomes everything.
