## Overview

The talk starts with what AI agents can do now. The tools that clinicians and families already carry on their phones, ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, can remember what they've been told, plan a sequence of steps, act and check their own work. The evidence about them has also moved from written cases and exam questions to real patients: emergency departments, primary care, and a trial across 74 hospital units where an AI reading patterns in nurses' documentation lowered the risk of in-hospital death. Bean and colleagues' Oxford study complicates the picture in a useful way. The AI on its own named the right condition 95% of the time, but members of the public using the same AI managed under 35%, mostly because of what they left out and how they asked. Taking a history is still a clinical skill, whoever is asking the questions.

From there I turn to how we're responding. The worries clinicians tell me about are reasonable, but the usual responses to them, which I call sanctuary strategies, leave us watching something happen to us, and any argument built on what humans do better gives up ground with each new model. I'd rather ask what clinicians and AI could reach together, because that keeps our attention on the child and family.

The second half treats care as a system that already includes the family's AI. Families arrive having asked about the EEG report, the MRI and the prognosis, and some, like Lily's parents in the BBC story I open with, arrive with a name for the condition. I think of this as a new kind of health literacy, and it's early for families and for us, so the talk looks at how clinicians can help families ask open questions, give their AI useful context and be careful about what they share, and at what an MDT looks like when everyone brings their own agent. It closes with what this asks of clinical teams, and one thing to try tomorrow: asking the next family you see how they're using AI.
