> [!info] Most of what feels foundational is about sixty years old
> Peer review became standard in the 1960s. Disciplines hardened in the late nineteenth century. The research university is roughly 150 years old, and the premium on originality younger than that. Knowing when these arrangements arrived makes them arguable, which is the point.

## Scholarly norms are recent inventions

Arguments for changing how scholarship works usually run into an appeal to tradition: this is how knowledge has always been certified. It is worth knowing how little of it is old.

**Peer review is recent.** Before the 1960s and 70s, journal editors largely decided what to publish on their own judgement. Whatever peer review is, it is not the ancient guarantor of quality it gets invoked as — which makes experimenting with other forms of quality assurance a continuation of scholarly practice rather than an assault on it.

**Disciplines are modern.** The boundaries academics organise careers around barely existed before the late nineteenth century. Newton was a natural philosopher. The rigidity is an administrative achievement of the last 150 years, not a map of how knowledge is shaped.

**Citation began as humility.** Early scholarly citation acknowledged dependence on others rather than claiming priority or defending against plagiarism. Dwarfs on the shoulders of giants was a statement about debt, not about credit allocation.

**Universities taught first.** The research university is about 150 years old. Before it, faculty were teachers first and scholars second. Publish-or-perish is a policy, adopted recently, not a description of what a university is for.

**Originality is newer still.** The premium on original contribution emerged in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Before that, good scholarship meant skilfully arranging, commenting on and extending what already existed — closer to what [[Notes/emergent scholarship|emergent scholarship]] calls integration than to what a REF panel calls a contribution to knowledge.

**Journals began as letters.** The first scientific journals in the 1660s were formalised correspondence: scholars circulating work in progress to colleagues they trusted. Sharing before certification is the older practice; withholding until publication is the innovation.

**Scholarship was oral and dialogical.** Before print, formal disputation was the main venue for scholarly work. Written texts opened conversations rather than closing them.

**The linear pipeline is the newest invention of all.** Gap, question, method, approval, data, analysis, write-up, submission, citation count: that sequence describes mid-twentieth-century research administration, not the process by which anyone has ever had an idea. It arrived with the research grant and the audit, and it has since been mistaken for the thing it was built to monitor. The methods section is where the mistake is maintained — a document that reconstructs a [[Notes/emergence|complex]] process as a line, written after the fact by people who know it was not one.

## Why this matters

None of this is an argument that the older arrangements were better. Peer review, for all its faults, catches things; disciplines concentrate expertise in useful ways.

The argument is narrower, and it is about permission. When a norm is treated as constitutive of scholarship, proposing an alternative reads as leaving scholarship. When it is understood as a particular arrangement adopted at a particular moment for particular reasons, proposing an alternative is ordinary scholarly work — and the question becomes the useful one: are the conditions that justified it still in place?

For several of these, they are not. Distribution is no longer scarce, which was much of the case for gatekeeping. Problems no longer respect disciplinary boundaries. Coordination across a network is no longer expensive. The arrangements built for the older constraints are still running.

The last point carries the most weight for [[Notes/emergent scholarship|emergent scholarship]]. If the linear pipeline is a recent administrative overlay rather than a description of scholarly work, then noticing that the work is actually complex and unplannable is not a radical proposal. It is a return to how scholarship was understood for most of its history — dialogical, recombinant, and unembarrassed about arranging existing things into something new.
