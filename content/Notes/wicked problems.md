> [!info] Wicked means resistant to resolution, not evil
> A wicked problem cannot be definitively formulated, has no point at which it is finished, and admits solutions that are good or bad rather than true or false. Rittel and Webber (1973) named them to describe what planners kept running into when working in complex social systems. The category matters because the big problems worth working on are almost all of this kind, and because the standard response (define, solve, and close) is what turns a difficult problem into a wicked one.

## Wicked problems

**One-sentence definition:** A problem that resists resolution because its requirements are incomplete, contradictory, and changing, and because any attempt to address one aspect reveals or creates others.

Rittel and Webber (1973) set out ten characteristics. All ten, in their order:

1. **There is no definitive formulation.** How you state the problem already contains a theory of its solution. Different stakeholders state it differently, and each statement is legitimate.
2. **There is no stopping rule.** Nothing tells you the work is complete; you stop because you have run out of time, money, or patience.
3. **Solutions are not true or false, but good or bad.** There is no test that settles it, which is why disagreement persists among reasonable people who are all reasoning well. (Rittel and Webber's own phrase is "good-or-bad"; the common gloss "better or worse" softens it.)
4. **There is no immediate and no ultimate test of a solution.** Every intervention sets off consequences that keep unfolding, and some of the later ones will outweigh the intended ones.
5. **Every solution is a one-shot operation.** You cannot trial an intervention on a health system without changing the system you were trying to learn about, so there is no learning by trial and error.
6. **There is no enumerable set of potential solutions.** Nobody can list the options and pick the best, because the list is never complete and new options keep being invented.
7. **Every wicked problem is essentially unique.** There is enough shared structure to recognise the type of problem but never enough to transfer the solution from one to another.
8. **Every wicked problem can be seen as a symptom of another problem.** Ask why a problem exists and the answer is another problem: students disengage because the curriculum is overloaded, which is because accreditation demands it, which is because the profession fears risk, and so on. Each "why" moves you up a level, and there is no level at which the chain stops. Where you choose to intervene is a judgement, not a finding.
9. **The discrepancy can be explained in many ways, and the explanation chosen determines the resolution.** Poor NSS scores can be read as a teaching problem, a communication problem, an estates problem, or a cohort problem, and each reading points to a different intervention. The choice of explanation is where most of the politics happens.
10. **The planner has no right to be wrong.** Consequences land on people, so the scientist's licence to be provisionally mistaken, to publish a hypothesis and let others refute it, is not available. This is the cruellest of the ten.

## The relationship to complexity

Complexity science describes systems with open boundaries, non-linear cause and effect, feedback loops, and dependence on their own history (see [[Notes/emergence|emergence]]). The wicked-problems literature describes what happens when someone tries to solve a problem inside such a system. They are the same phenomenon seen from two positions. A complexity theorist looking at a health system sees feedback and non-linearity; a planner trying to change that health system experiences those same properties as a problem that will not hold still, cannot be tested, and turns out to be a symptom of something else. Rittel and Webber's ten characteristics are the view from the planner's chair.

That is why the two literatures, complexity science and the wicked-problems tradition in planning and public policy, keep arriving at the same recommendation from different starting points: iterative, contextualised, incremental action that shifts a pattern over time, in place of a better specification of the problem (Head & Alford, 2015; Fraser & Greenhalgh, 2001).

## Wickedness can be manufactured

The usual reading of wicked problems treats wickedness as a property of the terrain: the problem is hard because the world is complicated, and better management would get us closer to a solution. Eoyang and Mennin (2019) argue the reverse for health professions education. Every institution, clinical setting, and student differs, and the field responds to that variability by intensifying standardisation, rigour, and accountability to competencies in every facet. Their conclusion is that *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.* On this reading some of the wickedness is produced by the management itself, by insisting on uniformity in a system whose variability was never a defect. Cleland, Patterson, and Hanson (2018) make a parallel argument about selection and widening access, problems that have absorbed decades of effort while remaining unresolved, as wicked problems always are.

## What to do instead of aiming for solutions

Roberts (2000) identifies three coping strategies, each with a real cost:

**Authoritative** strategies tame the problem by putting it in the hands of a few. Reducing the number of stakeholders genuinely reduces complexity, because the competing perspectives are eliminated at the start. But those perspectives were part of the problem, so this is also a failure mode.

**Competitive** strategies set opposing positions against each other and pick the best. Solutions get compared. But the adversarial framing discourages the knowledge-sharing that would have produced better ones.

**Collaborative** strategies engage all stakeholders to find something that works for all of them. Slower, and dependent on a level of trust that often is not there. This is the only one of the three that does not achieve tractability by discarding part of the problem.

Networks matter here for a structural reason: any problem sufficiently complex to be worth caring about is one you can't address alone (Conklin, 2005).

## Why this is worth your attention

Wicked problems are typically where the consequential work of health professions education is: health inequalities, multimorbidity across fragmented services, the health consequences of climate change, and the integration of care across institutional boundaries. None of these has a solution, all reward sustained attention, and none can be addressed by someone waiting for a tractable version to appear.

They also carry a career implication. The work that moves a wicked problem forward is rarely the work that produces clean outputs on the schedule an institution counts. That is a real cost of choosing them, not a reason to avoid them.
