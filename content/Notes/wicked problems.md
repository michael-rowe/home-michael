> [!info] Wicked means resistant to resolution, not evil
> A wicked problem cannot be definitively formulated, has no point at which it is finished, and admits solutions that are better or worse rather than true or false. Rittel and Webber named them in 1973 to describe what planners kept running into. The category matters because the big problems worth working on are almost all in it, and because the standard response — define, solve, close — makes them worse.

## Wicked problems

**One-sentence definition:** A problem that resists resolution because its requirements are incomplete, contradictory and changing, and because any attempt to address one aspect reveals or creates others.

Rittel and Webber set out ten characteristics. The ones that do most of the work:

- **No definitive formulation.** How you state the problem already contains a theory of its solution. Different stakeholders state it differently and each statement is legitimate.
- **No stopping rule.** You do not finish; you run out of time, money or patience.
- **Solutions are better or worse, not true or false.** There is no test that settles it, which is why disagreement persists among people who are all reasoning well.
- **Every solution is a one-shot operation.** You cannot trial an intervention on a health system without changing the system you were trying to learn about.
- **Every wicked problem is a symptom of another problem.** Ask why and you move up a level indefinitely.
- **Every wicked problem is essentially unique.** Enough shared structure to recognise the type; never enough to transfer the solution.

The "social planner has no right to be wrong" is the last one, and the cruellest. Consequences land on people, so the usual scientific licence to be provisionally mistaken is not available.

## The relationship to complexity

A wicked problem is what a [[Notes/emergence|complex system]] looks like from the position of someone who wants a solution. The characteristics are not a separate phenomenon; they are what open boundaries, non-linearity, feedback and history-dependence feel like when you try to act.

This is why the two literatures keep converging, and why the response is the same in both: not better specification, but iterative, contextualised, incremental action that shifts a pattern over time.

## Wickedness can be manufactured

Eoyang and Mennin make the sharpest version of this point about health professions education. Every institution, clinical setting and student differs — and the field responds by intensifying standardisation, rigour and accountability to competencies in every facet. Their conclusion: *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.*

That inverts the usual reading. The wickedness is not an unfortunate property of the terrain that better management would overcome. Some of it is produced by the management — by insisting on uniformity in a system whose variability is not a defect. Cleland and colleagues make a parallel argument about selection and widening access, problems that have absorbed decades of effort while remaining exactly as unresolved as wicked problems always are.

## What to do instead of solving

Roberts identifies three coping strategies, each with a real cost:

**Authoritative** strategies tame the problem by putting it in the hands of a few. Reducing the number of stakeholders genuinely reduces complexity, because the competing perspectives are eliminated at the start — which is also the failure mode, since those perspectives were part of the problem.

**Competitive** strategies set opposing positions against each other and pick the best. Solutions get compared; the adversarial framing discourages the knowledge-sharing that would have produced better ones.

**Collaborative** strategies engage all stakeholders to find something that works for all of them. Slower, and dependent on a level of trust that often is not there — but the only one of the three that does not achieve tractability by discarding part of the problem.

Networks matter here for a structural reason rather than a sentimental one: any problem sufficiently complex to be worth caring about is one you cannot address alone.

## Why this is worth your attention

Wicked problems are where the consequential work is. Health inequalities, multimorbidity across fragmented services, the health consequences of climate change, integration of care across institutional boundaries — none has a solution, all reward sustained attention, and none can be addressed by someone waiting for a tractable version to appear.

They also carry a career implication worth being honest about. The work that moves a wicked problem forward is rarely the work that produces clean outputs on the schedule an institution counts. That is a real cost of choosing them, not a reason to avoid them.
