> [!info] Scholarship as a complex adaptive system, and what follows from treating it as one
> A conversation with a colleague, a seagull stealing a chip, three paragraphs of a paper, a tweet you disagree with, and the premise of a science fiction novel can combine into one infographic that says something true. The formal account of scholarship has no place for that process, because it assumes the output was planned. Emergent scholarship starts from the position that it couldn't have been.

## Emergent scholarship

**One-sentence definition:** An account of scholarly work in all four of its forms — discovery, integration, application, and teaching — as a complex adaptive system working to move wicked problems forward, in which contributions emerge from interactions that cannot be specified in advance.

This is an argument, and I want to be clear about that from the start. The claim is that scholarship behaves this way and that most of the apparatus built to manage it assumes otherwise. What follows is what the claim implies if it holds, and the connections to complexity science were looked for on purpose.

The adjective carries a technical meaning. *Emergent* is not a synonym for new, informal, open, or digital; it means what it means in complexity science, a property of the whole that is present in none of the parts, arising from their interaction, and unpredictable from the parts considered separately. See [[Notes/emergence|emergence]].

## The claim

The scholarly system is administered as though scholarship were complicated, in the sense of a decomposable process that can be written out as steps. Identify a gap, formulate a question, select a method, obtain approval, collect data, analyse, write up, submit, revise, publish, count citations. Every stage can be specified in advance, audited afterwards, and reported to someone. As a description of the administration that is accurate enough. As a description of the work it misses almost everything.

Consider how a contribution actually arrives. Something a colleague said over coffee sits unresolved for a month. A seagull takes a chip off a table in front of you and you notice something about opportunism and reward. You read three paragraphs in a paper about something else entirely. A tweet annoys you enough that you work out precisely why. The premise of a novel supplies the shape. These collide, and what comes out is an infographic, a form none of the inputs suggested, making a claim that none of them contains.

Ask where in the scholarly system that process is accounted for. There is no stage for the seagull. The ethics application required you to state your question before you had it, the project plan required outputs you could not have named, and the methods section, written afterwards, reconstructs the whole thing as a line. Everyone who writes one knows it is a reconstruction.

What happened there is emergence in the technical sense. The output was in none of the inputs and could not have been derived from them by any procedure. It depended on history, because it needed your particular years of reading to become the thing it became, and it was non-linear, because the seagull contributed more than the paper did. Its boundaries cannot be drawn, since there is no saying where the relevant system ends, which is why nobody can tell you in advance what to read.

## What follows from taking this seriously

If scholarship is complex, then the useful question shifts from what the steps are to what conditions make good collisions likely. You can't plan an emergent outcome, but you can build a system with more surface area for encounter, keep heterogeneous material in one place long enough for it to interact, resist organising it prematurely into categories that foreclose the connections you haven't had yet, and make it cheap to produce an output in whatever form the idea turns out to need.

Innovation is recombination, so the work is to find many ideas and give yourself many chances to recombine them. That is why [[Notes/principles of emergent scholarship|the principles]] are stated as *X through Y*: each names a condition rather than a step.

It also explains why the shape of the output can't be fixed in advance. If a claim arrives wanting to be an infographic, a piece of software, a recorded conversation, or a guide, forcing it into a journal article destroys the thing that emerged.

## AI agents add to the complexity

Introducing AI agents into scholarly work adds a new class of agent to the system, one that acquires behaviours through interaction which are, in Rahwan's (2019) phrase, virtually impossible to predict, and whose reasoning is frequently indecipherable even when it can be described. We don't fully control them and we don't fully understand them.

Read as a control problem that is alarming. Read as complexity it is the ordinary consequence of adding interacting agents to a system: more interaction, more non-linearity, less predictability, and in the same measure more opportunity for emergence. Since the agent's outputs can't be specified in advance, the productive response is to bound the context it works within, which can be. That is the argument behind the [[Notes/research harness|research harness]].

## The limits of the claim

This is an argument about the limits of control, and it says nothing about the limits of knowledge.

Philosophy of science has long separated the context of discovery from the context of justification: how you arrived at a claim, and whether the claim is warranted. Everything above concerns the first. The seagull explains where the idea came from and contributes nothing to whether the idea is true. That second question is answered the way it has always been answered, by evidence, argument, and other people checking, and an unplannable route to a claim does nothing to defend it. If anything the obligation runs harder the other way: the less procedural your path, the more the warrant has to carry, because the procedure is not there to carry any of it for you.

The claim is also bounded. Complexity is a property of particular systems, and plenty of scholarship is legitimately procedural and should stay that way: a trial protocol, a systematic review, a psychometric validation, a replication. These are complicated, they reward specification, and treating them as emergent would be an error of exactly the kind this note warns against in the other direction. Rockets get to Mars. Some things can be predicted with high certainty, and where they can be, predicting them is the work.

What the argument does say is narrower. For a significant part of scholarly work, sense-making depends on more interacting variables than anyone can track, and the system's insistence that this part be planned, declared, and audited in advance produces paperwork rather than knowledge.

This has been said from inside health professions education. Eoyang and Mennin (2019) observe that every institution, setting, and student differs, and that the field responds by intensifying standardisation and accountability in every facet, with the result that *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.* Their response is to change the shape of action rather than abandon it: contextualised, iterative, incremental moves that shift a pattern over time.

## Three things it isn't

It isn't a technology story. Git, static sites, and AI are infrastructure, and the argument survives them. It isn't an objective account; it is an opinionated one, worked out in public. And it isn't a claim that nobody already works this way. Most good scholars do, and then write the methods section that hides it. What is missing is a system that recognises the process it depends on.
