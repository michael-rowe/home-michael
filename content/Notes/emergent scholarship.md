> [!info] Scholarship as a complex adaptive system, and what follows from treating it as one
> Something a student said in a debrief, an electrician explaining a job to an apprentice in your kitchen, three paragraphs of a paper, a consultation document you disagree with, and the premise of a science fiction novel can combine into one short guide for practice educators that says something true. The formal account of scholarship has no place for that, because it assumes the output was planned. Emergent scholarship starts from the position that it couldn't have been.

## Emergent scholarship

**One-sentence definition:** An account of scholarly work in all four of [[Notes/Boyer's model of scholarship|Boyer's forms]] — discovery, integration, application, and teaching — as a [[Notes/scholarship as a complex adaptive system|complex adaptive system]] working to move [[Notes/wicked problems|wicked problems]] forward, in which contributions emerge from interactions that cannot be specified in advance.

*Emergent* is not a synonym for new, informal, open, or digital; it means what it means in complexity science, a property of the whole that is present in none of the parts, arising from their interaction, and unpredictable from the parts considered separately. See [[Notes/emergence|emergence]].

## The claim

The scholarly system is administered as though scholarship were complicated, in the sense of a process you can decompose and write out as steps. Identify a gap, formulate a question, select a method, obtain approval, collect data, analyse, write up, submit, revise, publish, count citations. Every stage can be specified in advance, audited afterwards, and reported to someone, which is a fair description of the administration and a poor description of the work.

Consider how a contribution actually arrives. Something a student said in a debrief sits unresolved for a month. An electrician rewiring your kitchen talks an apprentice through the job, and you notice how much of what he's doing is narration rather than instruction. You read three paragraphs in a paper about handovers in air traffic control. A consultation document annoys you enough that you work out precisely why. The premise of a novel supplies the shape. These collide, and what comes out is a short guide for practice educators, a form none of the inputs suggested, making a claim that none of them contains.

There's nowhere in the scholarly system to put any of that. The ethics application asked for your question before you knew what it really was, the project plan asked for outputs you couldn't have named because you didn't know what format would best convey the findings, and the methods section, written afterwards, lays the whole thing out as a straight line that anyone who has written one knows is a reconstruction. There is no line on the form for the electrician.

What happened in the process described above is emergence in the technical sense. The output was in none of the inputs and couldn't have been derived from them by any procedure. It depended on history, because it needed your particular years of reading to become the thing it became, and it was non-linear, because the electrician contributed more than the paper did. Its boundaries can't be drawn either, since there's no saying where the relevant system ends, which is why nobody can tell you in advance what to read.

## What follows when we take this seriously

If scholarship is complex, the useful question shifts from what the steps are to what conditions make good collisions likely. You can't plan an emergent outcome, but you can build a system with more surface area for encounter, keep heterogeneous material in one place long enough for it to interact, resist organising it prematurely into categories that foreclose connections you haven't had yet, and make it cheap to produce an output in whatever form the idea turns out to need.

Innovation is recombination, so the work is to gather many ideas and give yourself many chances to recombine them. That's why [[Notes/principles of emergent scholarship|the principles]] are stated as *X through Y*: each one names a condition rather than a step.

It also explains why the shape of the output can't be fixed in advance. If a claim arrives wanting to be an infographic, a piece of software, a recorded conversation, or a guide, forcing it into a journal article destroys the thing that emerged.

## AI agents add to the complexity

Introducing AI agents into scholarly work adds a new class of agent to the system, one that acquires behaviours through interaction which are, in Rahwan's (2019) phrase, virtually impossible to predict, and whose reasoning is often indecipherable even when it can be described. We don't fully control them and we don't fully understand them.

Read as a control problem, that's alarming. Read as complexity, it's the ordinary consequence of adding interacting agents to a system: more interaction, more non-linearity, less predictability, and in the same measure more opportunity for emergence. The agent's outputs can't be specified in advance, but the context it works within can be, and bounding that context is the argument behind the [[Notes/research harness|research harness]].

## The limits of the claim

This is an argument about the limits of control, and it says nothing about the limits of knowledge.

Philosophy of science has long separated the context of discovery from the context of justification: how you arrived at a claim, and whether the claim is warranted. Everything above concerns the first. The electrician explains where the idea came from and contributes nothing to whether the idea is true. That second question gets answered the way it has always been answered, by evidence, argument, and other people checking, and an unplannable route to a claim does nothing to defend it. If anything the obligation runs harder the other way: the less procedural your path, the more the warrant has to carry, because the procedure isn't there to carry any of it for you.

The claim is bounded in another way too. Complexity is a property of particular systems, and plenty of scholarship is legitimately procedural and should stay that way: a trial protocol, a systematic review, a psychometric validation, a replication. These are complicated rather than complex, they reward specification, and treating them as emergent would be an error of exactly the kind this note warns against in the other direction. Some things can be predicted with high certainty, rockets get to Mars, and where they can be, predicting them is the work.

The argument is narrower than that. For a significant part of scholarly work, sense-making depends on more interacting variables than anyone can track, and the system's insistence that this part be planned, declared, and audited in advance produces paperwork rather than knowledge.

That has been said from inside health professions education. Eoyang and Mennin (2019) observe that every institution, setting, and student differs, and that the field responds by intensifying standardisation and accountability in every facet, with the result that *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.* Their response is to change the shape of action rather than abandon it: contextualised, iterative, incremental moves that shift a pattern over time.

## Three things it isn't

It isn't a technology story. Git, static sites, and AI are infrastructure, and the argument survives them. It isn't an objective account either; it's an opinionated one, worked out in public, and the connections to complexity science were looked for on purpose. And it isn't a claim that nobody already works this way. Most good scholars do, and then write the methods section that hides it. What's missing is a system that recognises the process it depends on.
