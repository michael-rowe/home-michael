> [!info] Scholarship is complex, and we have been administering it as though it were merely complicated
> A reflection on a conversation with a colleague, a seagull stealing a chip, three paragraphs of a paper, a tweet you disagree with and the premise of a science fiction novel can combine into one infographic that says something true. Nothing in the formal account of scholarship can describe that process, because the formal account assumes the output was planned. Emergent scholarship starts from the position that it could not have been.

## Emergent scholarship

**One-sentence definition:** An account of scholarly work in all four of its forms — discovery, integration, application and teaching — as a complex adaptive system working to move wicked problems forward, in which contributions emerge from interactions that cannot be specified in advance.

This is an argument rather than a description of a natural order. The claim is that scholarship behaves this way and that most of the apparatus built to manage it assumes otherwise; what follows is what the claim implies if it holds.

The adjective is doing technical work. *Emergent* is not a synonym for new, informal, open, or digital. It means what it means in complexity science: a property of the whole that is present in none of the parts, arising from interaction, and not predictable from the parts considered separately. See [[Notes/emergence|emergence]].

## The claim

The scholarly system is administered as though scholarship were **complicated**: a decomposable process that can be written out as steps. Identify a gap. Formulate a question. Select a method. Obtain approval. Collect data. Analyse. Write up. Submit. Revise. Publish. Count citations. Every stage is specifiable in advance, auditable afterwards, and reportable to someone.

That description is not wrong about the administration. It is wrong about the work.

Consider how a contribution actually arrives. Something a colleague said over coffee sits unresolved for a month. A seagull takes a chip off a table in front of you and you notice something about opportunism and reward. You read three paragraphs in a paper about something else entirely. A tweet annoys you enough that you work out precisely why. The premise of a novel supplies the shape. These collide, and what comes out is an infographic — a form none of the inputs suggested, making a claim that none of them contains.

Ask where in the scholarly system that process is accounted for. There is no stage for the seagull. The ethics application required you to state your question before you had it. The project plan required outputs you could not have named. The methods section, written afterwards, reconstructs the whole thing as a line — and everyone who writes one knows it is a reconstruction.

**This is emergence, not sloppiness.** The output was not present in any input. It could not have been derived from them by a procedure. It is history-dependent: it needed your particular years of reading to be the thing it became. It is non-linear: the seagull contributed more than the paper did. Its boundaries are undefinable — you cannot say where the relevant system ends, which is why nobody can tell you in advance what to read.

## What follows from taking this seriously

If scholarship is complex, then the productive question is not *what are the steps* but *what conditions make good collisions likely*. You cannot plan an emergent outcome. You can build a system with more surface area for encounter, keep heterogeneous material in one place long enough for it to interact, resist organising it prematurely into categories that foreclose the connections you have not had yet, and make it cheap to produce an output in whatever form the idea turns out to need.

Innovation is recombination, so the work is to find many ideas and give yourself many chances to recombine them. This is why [[Notes/principles of emergent scholarship|the principles]] are stated as *X through Y* rather than as procedures: each names a condition, not a step.

It also explains why the shape of the output cannot be fixed in advance. If a claim arrives wanting to be an infographic, a piece of software, a recorded conversation or a guide, forcing it into the journal article format destroys the thing that emerged. The medium is not packaging applied after the fact.

## AI agents raise the complexity again

Introducing AI agents into scholarly work adds a new class of agents to the system — ones that acquire behaviours through interaction that are, in Iyad Rahwan's phrase, virtually impossible to predict, and whose reasoning is frequently indecipherable even when it can be described. We do not fully control them and we do not fully understand them.

Read as a control problem, this is alarming. Read as complexity, it is the ordinary consequence of adding interacting agents to a system: more interaction, more non-linearity, less predictability — and, in the same measure, more opportunity for emergence. The productive response is not to specify the agent's outputs in advance, which cannot be done, but to bound the context it works within, which can. That is the argument behind the [[Notes/research harness|research harness]].

## The limits of the claim

This is an argument about the limits of control, not about the limits of knowledge — and the difference is the whole of it.

Philosophy of science has long separated the **context of discovery** from the **context of justification**: how you arrived at a claim, and whether the claim is warranted. Everything above concerns the first. The seagull explains where the idea came from; it contributes nothing to whether the idea is true. Those remain separate questions, and the second is answered the way it has always been answered — by evidence, argument, and other people checking.

So emergence does not promote opinion to knowledge. An unplannable route to a claim is not a defence of the claim. If anything the obligation runs harder the other way: the less procedural your path, the more the warrant has to carry, because the procedure is not there to carry any of it for you.

Nor is the claim universal. Complexity is a property of particular systems, not a mood. Plenty of scholarship is legitimately procedural and should stay that way — a trial protocol, a systematic review, a psychometric validation, a replication. These are complicated rather than complex, they reward specification, and treating them as emergent would be an error of exactly the kind this note warns against in the other direction. Rockets get to Mars. Some things can be predicted with high certainty, and where they can be, predicting them is the work.

What the argument does say is narrower: that for a significant part of scholarly work, sense-making depends on more interacting variables than anyone can track, and the system's insistence that this part be planned, declared and audited in advance produces paperwork rather than knowledge.

This has been said from inside health professions education. Eoyang and Mennin observe that every institution, setting and student differs, and that the field responds by intensifying standardisation and accountability in every facet — with the result that *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.* Their response is not to abandon action but to change its shape: contextualised, iterative, incremental moves that shift a pattern, rather than solutions that close a problem.

## What this is not

It is not a technology story. Git, static sites and AI are infrastructure; the argument survives them.

It is not an objective account. It is an opinionated one, worked out in public.

And it is not a claim that nobody already works this way. Most good scholars do — and then write the methods section that hides it. What is missing is a system that recognises the process it actually depends on.
