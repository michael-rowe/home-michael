> [!info] A rocket is complicated; a rainforest is complex
> Emergence is the appearance of properties at the level of a whole that are present in none of its parts. It is the reason some systems can be taken apart and understood while others cannot, and the reason that writing out the steps of a process is sometimes an accurate description and sometimes a fiction.

## Emergence

**One-sentence definition:** A property is emergent when it appears through the interaction of elements that do not individually possess it, and cannot be predicted from those elements considered separately (Pessoa, 2021).

Water is wet, and neither hydrogen nor oxygen is. The wetness appears when enough molecules interact, and there is no point in looking for it in any one of them. An ant has a small repertoire of general-purpose behaviours and no idea what a nest is, while the colony builds nests, forages, and defends itself, with a competence that exists only in the interaction between ants and nowhere else.

## Complicated and complex are different kinds of thing

A rocket is complicated. It has an enormous number of parts and understanding it is hard, but it can be decomposed: the parts can be specified, the interactions are known, and the behaviour of the whole follows from the behaviour of the parts. Given enough expertise you can write down the steps.

A rainforest is complex. You can't specify it by listing its components, because what it does arises from interactions that are non-linear, historically contingent, and continuously changing. There is no set of steps that adequately describes an end state or a target state for a rainforest.

The distinction matters because the two call for opposite responses. Complicated systems reward planning, specification, and control. Give a complex system the same treatment and you suppress the local adaptation it runs on, and end up with an elaborate plan describing something other than what is happening.

## Diagnosis is the skill

Neither category is the better one, and complexity is a property of particular systems rather than a condition the world is in. Most real work involves both, often in the same afternoon.

Getting the diagnosis wrong is a mistake in both directions. Treat a complex system as complicated and you produce procedures nobody can follow and plans that describe a fiction. Treat a complicated system as complex and you abandon work that was tractable, excuse yourself from rigour you could have exercised, and mistake "nobody can predict this" for a finding when it was a failure to do the analysis.

Rockets still reach Mars. Drug trials still establish what works. A great deal can be predicted with high certainty, and where it can be, it should be. The claim that some systems resist specification is an argument for spending the effort where it pays and recognising where it doesn't.

Stacey's (1996) matrix is one tool for making that call. It locates a situation on two dimensions, how much agreement exists about what to do and how much certainty exists about cause and effect, and produces four zones. Close to both, the situation is simple, past data predicts the future, and you plan and monitor against the plan. Far from one but not the other it is complicated, and the work is either political, negotiating agreement, or exploratory, holding a shared direction while the path stays open. Far from both it is complex, and what it needs is adaptability rather than better planning. Further still lies chaos. The matrix still asks for a judgement, but it makes the judgement explicit.

## Features that make a system complex

Holland (2014) lists the features that distinguish complex systems, and each one defeats a different part of the planning instinct:

- Many interacting elements, exchanging matter or information
- Rich interaction: everything is affected by, and affects, other things
- Non-linearity: small changes can produce large effects, and large efforts nothing at all
- Feedback: any interaction can loop back on itself
- Open or undefinable boundaries, so that it is often impossible to say where the system ends
- Operation far from equilibrium, so that organisation needs continuous energy to persist (Prigogine, 1978)
- History-dependence: the system's past is co-responsible for its present behaviour
- Local ignorance: elements respond to what is near them and not to the system as a whole

Taken together they mean the behaviour of the whole becomes available by watching it rather than by calculating it. Prigogine (1978) makes the point about history from inside physics: once a system passes through a bifurcation, its present state can only be interpreted through the path it took, "a historical element, which until now seemed to be reserved only for sciences dealing with biological, social, and cultural phenomena."

## Why this shows up in knowledge work

Knowledge is an emergent property of interaction. Understanding arrives through dialogue and exploration rather than through the transfer of a finished object, which is why a lecture and a conversation about the same content produce different results, and why a reader alone with a text can question an assumption but can't test it (Victor, 2011).

The same logic applies one level up, to how scholarly contributions come into being. See [[Notes/emergent scholarship|emergent scholarship]].
