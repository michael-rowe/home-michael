> [!info] You cannot write down the steps, because there are no steps
> Scholarship and professional education are sociotechnical complex adaptive systems: autonomous agents — people, institutions, technologies, and now AI — interacting in ways that produce system behaviour nobody specified. Managing them as though they were complicated, by writing procedures and auditing compliance, suppresses the adaptation they actually run on.

## Scholarship as a complex adaptive system

**One-sentence definition:** A lens that treats education and scholarship as systems of interdependent social and technical agents whose behaviour emerges from their interactions, rather than as machines whose outputs follow from their inputs.

This is the theoretical grounding underneath [[Notes/emergent scholarship|emergent scholarship]], and it earns its place by making a prediction the managerial account cannot: that procedures will keep failing in the same way, not because they were written badly, but because the system is the wrong kind of thing to write procedures for.

"Sociotechnical" matters more than the softer "ecosystem". It insists that technologies are agents in the system rather than neutral instruments used by it. A reference manager, an assessment platform, an AI tutor — each changes what is easy to do, therefore what gets done, therefore what the system comes to treat as normal.

## The non-decomposability problem

Complicated systems can be specified. Complex ones cannot, and for reasons that are structural rather than a matter of insufficient effort: interactions are non-linear, so effort and effect are uncoupled; boundaries are open, so you cannot say what is in the system; behaviour is history-dependent, so the same intervention does different things at different moments; and agents respond to local conditions in ignorance of the whole. See [[Notes/emergence|emergence]].

Every quality assurance instrument in higher education assumes the opposite. Learning outcomes declared in advance. Research questions fixed at ethics approval. Impact projected in a funding application. Curriculum mapped as though the mapping were the thing. Each is a reasonable request to make of a complicated system and an impossible one to make of a complex system, so what gets produced is a document that satisfies the request while the real work proceeds somewhere else. The gap between the two is not dishonesty. It is what happens when you audit a rainforest with a parts list.

Eoyang and Mennin put the same point more precisely, and from inside health professions education: every institution is different, every clinical setting unique, every student starting somewhere else — "and yet, we focus on the standardization, rigor, and accountability to competencies and curricula in every facet." Their conclusion is the one that matters here: *the search for consistency and control in such a highly variable system leads inevitably to intractable, wicked problems.* The wickedness is not bad luck or poor implementation. It is manufactured by the mismatch between the instrument and the system.

Wicked problems, in Rittel's original sense, have no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and solutions that are better or worse rather than true or false. What follows is not resignation but a different kind of action: contextualised, iterative, incremental moves that shift a persistent pattern over time, rather than solutions that close a problem.

## The tensions worth naming

These are the sites where the complexity is negotiated in practice. They are not defects awaiting a solution; they are where the system's adaptation comes from, and the productive management of tension — rather than its resolution — is what generates anything durable.

**Standardisation against personalisation.** Accreditation demands comparability; meaningful education requires adaptation to this learner in this context.

**Efficiency against relationship.** Technology promises scale, but learning is relational. The question is which feedback loops correct for what is lost, and whether any exist.

**Control against emergence.** Policy assumes predictability; learning is emergent and largely invisible to the instruments pointed at it.

**Regulation against innovation.** Regulation secures a floor and simultaneously locks behaviour in, which is why innovation tends to appear at the periphery and has to travel inward.

**Transmission against transformation.** Is education the transfer of stable knowledge, or the creation of conditions from which understanding and professional identity emerge? The two answers imply incompatible curricula and incompatible assessment.

## AI agents change the system, not just the tools

A tool does what it is told. An agent acts. AI agents acquire novel behaviours through interaction with their environment and with each other, and those behaviours are effectively impossible to predict — even when a solution can be described mathematically, the description can be too long and too tangled to interpret. This is why Rahwan argues they need studying as a class of actors with their own behavioural patterns, not only as artefacts of engineering.

Adding them to scholarship adds agents, interactions and feedback loops to a system that was already complex. Three things follow.

The system becomes less predictable, which is a real cost and should be said plainly. It also becomes more generative: more interaction is more opportunity for emergence, and some of what comes back is good in ways nobody could have specified. And the locus of control shifts. You cannot specify an agent's outputs in advance; you can bound the context it operates within — what it can see, how it may reason, what it may do without asking. That is the move the [[Notes/research harness|research harness]] makes, and it is a complexity-appropriate response rather than a compliance one.

## The opposite failure

Everything above argues against treating a complex system as complicated. The mirror error is just as available and, in this literature, more common: reaching for complexity to explain why something could not be planned, measured or accounted for, when the honest answer is that the analysis was not done.

Complexity language is easy to use decoratively. "The system is complex" can be made to excuse any absence of evidence, any unevaluated intervention, any claim that resists checking. It can also be used to dodge the parts of education that genuinely are tractable — whether a curriculum covers what it claims, whether an assessment measures what it says, whether an intervention outperformed the alternative. Those are answerable questions and complexity is not a reason to stop answering them.

This is documented rather than hypothetical. Cristancho, Field and Lingard found complexity becoming a "god term" in medical education, and identified four patterns across eighteen years of published work: complexity is described in a variety of incompatible ways; multiple orientations get combined inside a single paper; the orientation in use is usually left implicit; and it is cited through secondary sources rather than primary ones. Their point is not that complexity is the wrong lens. It is that there are several legitimate complexity sciences with distinct disciplinary roots, and using the word without saying which one you mean produces the appearance of theory rather than theory.

The test is whether the lens constrains you. A framework that explains every outcome equally well after the fact explains nothing, and complexity is unusually prone to this. If invoking it never rules anything out, it is being used as cover.

## What to do instead of planning

Nudge and watch. Change what is easy, change what is visible, change who is in the room, and observe what comes back. Small experiments in contained contexts, documented openly, beat institutional strategies that assume the destination is knowable.

Tolerate mess. Half-formed material that has not been filed is the substrate emergence works on; premature organisation forecloses the connections you had not thought of yet. Systems that demand everything be categorised on arrival optimise for retrieval and starve discovery.

### What remains unclear

Where the boundary falls in a given case — whether you are looking at a complex system or a complicated one you have not analysed hard enough. The Stacey matrix offers one instrument for this, locating a situation by how much agreement and how much certainty are available, and distinguishing simple, complicated, complex and chaotic zones with different appropriate responses. It is a useful discipline, though it asks you to judge certainty in advance, which is close to the thing being disputed.

The larger gap is a test: what does this lens forecast that a managerial account does not, and did it happen? The non-decomposability prediction above is a start. It has not been checked properly.
