### Abstract

The text-entry field and the chronological chat history have become the default interface for interacting with generative AI, and their familiarity has obscured how recent and contingent this arrangement is. This essay argues that chat is one interface among many, and a poor fit for the specific work of learning — work that unfolds over time, leaves traces, and requires the development of judgement. I extend into the AI interface the critique that Cormier, Morris and Stommel, and Downes articulated for earlier educational technologies — and move past it. Drawing on the tradition running from Bush's Memex through Nelson's hypertext to the contemporary tools-for-thought community, I propose that a more appropriate substrate for AI-supported learning already exists: the personal, version-controlled knowledge graph. A personal PKM vault is not a graph database, and the difference matters. It captures emergent, idiosyncratic structure as it develops, and its diff history preserves the working out that polished outputs discard. A local Model Context Protocol layer can expose such a vault — alongside other personal resources — to AI tools turning the learner's own environment into the substrate from which dialogue proceeds. Multiple interfaces can sit over this substrate, chat included. The contribution here is pedagogical: an argument for choosing substrates and interfaces that preserve the traces of becoming, and for treating the evidence of learning as recoverable from the process rather than inferred from its final artefact.

> ...chatbots are an odd way to interact with an AI. It is as if the only way for us to do work is through texting an intern… except you are texting a different intern in every chat, one who forgets everything you had previously discussed, and whose memory starts to fail after just a couple of pages of text.
>
> [Ethan Mollick](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/freeing-the-chatbot)

> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - The chat interface is one possible wrapper for generative AI, not the natural or inevitable one, and its dominance has obscured the question of what an interface for *learning* specifically might look like.
> - Learning leaves traces — drafts, revisions, connections made and unmade, ideas tried and abandoned — and the substrates we choose either preserve those traces or discard them.
> - A personal, version-controlled knowledge graph of the kind built in tools like Obsidian preserves the traces and makes them navigable. It is not a graph database, and conflating the two weakens both arguments.
> - A local Model Context Protocol layer can expose such a vault, alongside reference libraries, custom agents, and other personal resources, to AI tools in ways that make the vault itself — rather than any single interface — the substrate for learning.
> - Multiple interfaces can sit over this substrate: spatial canvases, notebooks, anchored annotation, structured forms, code editors, timelines, and chat. The design question is which interface preserves the traces that matter for the work being done.
> - Evidence of learning can then be interrogated through diff-based queries, link archaeology, orphan analysis, temporal rhythm, cross-referencing against cited literature, and grounded conversation — all things no human examiner could do at scale, and all things AI can now do with a navigable process record.

## The wrong interface for the work

The text-entry field and the chat history arrived as the simplest possible wrapper for a system that takes text and returns text. No one designed them for learning, or for any other specific kind of work. Familiarity has since done the rest. Within three years of ChatGPT's release, the text box has become so thoroughly naturalised as the way one interacts with AI that the question of whether it is the right interface for any particular purpose now has to be argued against a default that feels self-evident. It is not self-evident. It is a historical contingency that has been mistaken for an affordance.

This essay asks what an interface — or more precisely, what a stack of substrates and interfaces — might look like if we designed it for the specific work of learning. Learning is a kind of work with particular properties. It unfolds over years rather than minutes. It leaves traces in the form of drafts, revisions, notes, attempted connections, and abandoned threads. It produces a developmental arc that is at least as important as any single output. The evidence that learning has occurred lives in those traces — and the polished artefact that conventionally serves as its proxy discards them. The question is whether the substrates and interfaces we are currently building for AI-supported learning preserve that evidence or discard it.

This piece is one of three related essays addressing different parts of the same architectural question. A companion essay on [[curriculum-infrastructure|curriculum infrastructure]] argues that institutional knowledge — curricula, competencies, prerequisite chains — belongs in a graph database exposed through standardised interfaces, because institutional structure is the kind of knowledge that benefits from typed, schema-constrained representation. A companion essay on [[2026-04-11-phd-becoming-ai-assessment|the PhD as a process of becoming]] argues that the thesis has functioned as a failing proxy for an identity shift that was never really what was being assessed, and that the evidence of that shift is recoverable from the process rather than the document. This essay sits between them, making the case that individual learner knowledge belongs in a different kind of substrate — one that is emergent, personal, and version-controlled — and that a local tech stack built around that substrate can support AI-mediated learning in ways a chat interface cannot.

## The container schema, briefly

The critique of bounded educational spaces has been made many times, and I will not make it again at length here. Lakoff and Johnson's (1980) container schema — the cognitive tendency to understand experience in terms of bounded regions with interiors, exteriors, and things inside them — has structured educational design for centuries, running from textbooks through classrooms to learning management systems. Cormier (2008), Morris and Stommel (2015), Siemens (2005), and Downes (2005) have variously argued that this organising metaphor constrains what is pedagogically possible by severing the boundary-spanning connections where integrative insight develops. The critique is sound and the literature is substantial.

My specific extension is that the chat interface reproduces this schema in a new medium. A chat thread is a bounded region: related ideas are separated by *when* they were discussed rather than *how* they relate, and the conversation is enclosed within a thread that has no principled connection to anything outside it. When platforms organise these threads into folders, projects, or workspaces, the meta-structure perpetuates the fragmentation rather than resolving it. The substrate the interface sits on top of is a sequence of temporal containers, and the polished outputs those containers produce hide the process by which they were generated.

This matters in two ways. The first is that information enclosed in linear conversations imposes a cognitive reintegration burden that parallels the one compartmentalised curricula impose on learners. The second, and for this essay the more important, is that the substrate discards the traces of its own construction. A chat thread preserves what was said; it does not preserve the interrogation, the revision, or the development that led to anyone's understanding changing. The argument that follows turns on whether we can build substrates that do preserve those traces, and whether we can build interfaces that make them navigable. Both questions have been answered before, and from within the computing tradition rather than the educational one.

## The lineage we are extending

The idea that bounded documents misrepresent the connectedness of ideas is older than the web. Bush's (1945) Memex proposed associative trails as the structure of how thinking actually works, against the hierarchical organisation that existing information systems imposed. Nelson's (1965) hypertext vision made the claim more radical still: that the document itself is an accident of paper, and that the natural form of written thought is a network of linked fragments that readers traverse rather than consume linearly. Engelbart (1962) framed the whole project as augmenting human intellect through symbol manipulation systems that worked with, rather than against, the associative structure of cognition. These arguments predate the critiques of bounded educational design by decades, and they were made from within the computing tradition rather than the educational one.

Novak's concept mapping work (Novak & Cañas, 2008) brought the same impulse into educational research, producing forty years of empirical literature on graph-based knowledge representation as a pedagogical tool. Buckingham Shum and Okada's knowledge cartography research extended this into dialogue mapping and argument visualisation. Each of these traditions has been doing some version of what I want to argue for here, and naming them matters. The argument's novelty, if it has any, lies in the claim that these principles now have infrastructure that makes them practically achievable.

The web itself is the proof of concept that has been sitting in plain view for thirty years. The web is a graph. It became the most successful personal learning environment ever built because its underlying structure matches the associative character of how people actually pursue understanding — a consequence of design decisions made for other reasons. Every hyperlink is an explicit connection; every page is a node that can be traversed from many directions. That anyone ever thought a learning management system with a folder hierarchy was an improvement on this is one of the stranger historical accidents of educational technology.

The contemporary development that matters most for this essay is the tools-for-thought community: Obsidian, Roam Research, Tana, Logseq, and the practitioners who have been building on them — Andy Matuschak's work on evergreen notes and transformative tools for thought, Maggie Appleton's writing on digital gardens, the broader personal knowledge management community that has been prototyping this alternative in production for years with real users. This community has been doing something the educational literature has largely not: treating the graph as a substrate that individuals build over time and use directly. The vault precedes the view. The argument I am making about substrates and interfaces is one this community has been making implicitly the whole time.

## Substrate and interface

A substrate is the underlying representation in which knowledge and its development are stored. An interface is the way a particular user sees and works with that substrate for a particular task. Keeping these apart dissolves most of the apparent objections to this argument — including the cognitive load concerns that an earlier version ran into by conflating them. That version argued for a "graph-based user interface," presenting the graph as what the learner would see and navigate, and then had to defend against concerns about visual complexity that any honest reading of Sweller's work would raise. The conflation was the problem.

A richly structured substrate can accumulate over years without ever being presented to a novice as a complex visual graph. Different interfaces can sit on top of the same substrate, surfacing different views appropriate to different users, tasks, and stages of development. A curriculum lead working on programme design might want a network view showing prerequisite chains; a student planning their next week of study might want a kanban board showing cards grouped by stage; a quality assurance officer might want tabular output from a query; an examiner might want a timeline of diffs. The underlying typed relationships — or, for the personal case this essay will develop, the underlying linked notes and version history — are the same. The interface is what changes.

My colleagues and I are building something like this at the institutional level through the Vertex project, where a graph database holds curriculum structure and different user interfaces — including a kanban view whose cards are graph nodes in different states — sit on top of it. The example is imperfect because a kanban is not itself a graph, but that is exactly the point. The kanban is an interface choice, suited to a particular task, sitting on a substrate that remains navigable in other ways. The substrate is the thing that has to preserve the structure; the interface is the thing that has to suit the work. Once this distinction is in place, the question of which interface is "best" for AI-supported learning becomes ill-formed. There is no best interface. There are substrates that preserve traces and substrates that discard them, and there are interfaces that suit particular kinds of work and interfaces that do not.

## The personal knowledge graph as substrate for learning

A personal knowledge graph built in something like Obsidian is not a graph database, and this distinction is load-bearing. A graph database has a schema defined in advance, typed relationships with specified semantics, and an ontology that imposes structure on whatever goes into it. Its value comes from queryability against that fixed structure: if you know in advance what kinds of relationships matter, a graph database lets you traverse them with precision. This is the right substrate for institutional knowledge — curricula, competencies, compliance frameworks — and the [[curriculum-infrastructure|curriculum infrastructure essay]] makes that case in detail.

A personal PKM vault is the opposite. Its structure is emergent, idiosyncratic, and untyped, or typed only as much as the individual chooses to type it through tags, YAML frontmatter, and conventions they establish for themselves. Plain wikilinks in Obsidian carry no semantics beyond "these two notes are connected in some way the author thought was worth marking." Richer typing is available — through YAML properties and tools like Dataview — but it is optional and learner-controlled rather than schema-enforced. The value of the vault does not come from queryability against a fixed schema. It comes from the fact that the structure grows with the learner's understanding rather than being imposed on it in advance. A vault is a record of what one person has come to think, in the particular connections they have come to draw, at a level of typing that matches what they have been able to articulate. For learning, this is exactly right. Professional understanding is not a fixed ontology being populated with examples; it is an emergent structure being constructed through experience, and the substrate that captures it should have the same properties.

YAML frontmatter and tagging sit in an interesting middle position. They give the learner a controllable degree of typing — enough structure to make the vault machine-navigable without forcing the rigidity of a schema. A note tagged with `#clinical-reasoning` and frontmatter specifying `maturity: developing` and `related-cases: [[Patient-A]], [[Patient-B]]` carries more structure than a plain note with wikilinks, and less than a typed entity in a graph database. The learner chooses where on that spectrum to sit, and can revise the choice over time as their thinking matures. The companion essay on [[documentation-as-infrastructure|documentation as infrastructure]] develops this point about machine-readable structure in more detail; the personal knowledge graph supports a kind of lightweight, learner-controlled semantic layer that would be both unnecessary in a conversation and impossible in a chat history.

Version control is the move that turns a vault from a current state into evidence of becoming. Without version history, a personal knowledge graph is just a snapshot of what someone currently thinks. With git run locally — or a comparable version control pipeline — every committed state of every file is preserved and timestamped; link changes are recoverable by querying the diff history, and the record is as granular as the commit discipline. Obsidian's built-in file recovery plugin offers short-term snapshots for accidental deletion, not the longitudinal record this argument depends on. This is where the substrate stops being a static artefact and starts being a record of a process. The diff between last month's version of a note and this month's version shows where thinking changed. The commit history shows when connections were first drawn, when they were revised, when they were abandoned and when they were restored. The answer to the question the original version of this essay raised but could not answer — how does a graph-based environment make the *development* of expertise visible, not just its current state — is "through the diff history."

My own site is built from a vault of this kind. The notes are written in Obsidian, the wikilinks connect essays to supporting fragments and to each other, YAML frontmatter captures metadata and enables queries, the whole thing is version-controlled in git, pushed to GitHub, and published through Quartz by a GitHub Actions workflow that rebuilds the site whenever I commit. When a reader encounters an essay on the site, they are seeing one rendered view over a substrate that has other views: the graph visualisation Quartz generates, the tag pages, the local graph around any given note, the raw markdown in the repository, the git log of how any particular essay evolved. The essay they are reading is itself a node. I am aware of the rhetorical awkwardness of arguing for this architecture in a form whose connectedness only becomes visible once you leave the prose and look at the site that contains it, and I will return to this at the end. For now, the point is that the infrastructure for personal knowledge graphs as substrates for learning is not speculative. It exists, it works, and some of us are already living inside it.

## A personal MCP and the tech stack for learning

The substrate argument generalises once the Model Context Protocol enters the picture. MCP, introduced by Anthropic in late 2024, is a standardised way of exposing tools and data sources to AI models. Its significance for this essay is that it decouples the AI interface from the resources the AI can work with. A chatbot no longer has to be a destination where you go to ask questions; it can become one of several ways to interact with a tech stack that you control, where your own resources — your vault, your reference library, your custom prompts, your local databases — are available to the AI through a common protocol.

Consider what a personal MCP layer running locally might expose. An Obsidian vault, as a substrate of notes with version history. A Zotero library, as a structured bibliography with PDFs, annotations, and notes. A set of custom prompts or agentic harnesses written for particular kinds of work — reading a paper, drafting an argument, reviewing one's own recent notes. A local database of whatever structured information the individual wants available: a clinical case log, a set of teaching resources, a record of supervision meetings, a graph database holding a self-designed curriculum or any other structured process the learner has chosen to follow. A file system, a git repository, a note-taking app, a knowledge graph. All of these can be exposed through a common interface, all of them can become resources the AI draws on, and none of them has to live inside the chat window.

This changes what the chat interface is for. Once the substrate is separated from the interface, chat becomes one way among several of interacting with a stack that preserves state, context, and history across sessions. A learner might type into a chatbot for a quick query, use voice chat while walking to think through an idea, open their vault directly to add a note, drop into a Jupyter notebook to work through an analysis, and have all of these activities grounded in the same underlying set of resources. The AI is not forgetting between sessions because the state lives in the substrate rather than in the conversation. The conversation is ephemeral; the vault persists. Every interaction leaves a trace somewhere the learner can return to.

Chat is fine — voice chat in particular has real advantages for exploratory thinking that benefits from the low friction of speech. When it is the *only* interface the learner has, though, the substrate underneath it is a sequence of threads that discard everything the learner needs to retain. With a personal MCP layer in place, chat can do what it is good at — conversational exploration, quick queries, voice-mediated thinking — while the persistent work accumulates somewhere else. The learner's environment becomes the substrate. Chat is one doorway into it. The argument is for a tech stack with multiple doorways, all opening onto a substrate that preserves traces — one where chat is one way in, and the thread is not the only thing that persists.

## Alternative interfaces as views on substrates that preserve traces

The unifying principle is that an interface is suited to learning when the substrate it works with preserves traces and when AI can navigate those traces. Different domains will want different substrates and different interfaces, and the design question is whether this combination of substrate and interface preserves the working out Several alternatives are already in use in the tools-for-thought and adjacent communities, and each is worth naming briefly as a point of reference for what the larger space looks like.

Spatial canvases — tldraw, Excalidraw, Heptabase, Scrapbox — treat the workspace as an infinite two-dimensional plane where notes, images, and AI-generated artefacts can be arranged, grouped, and connected through proximity rather than typed edges. Clusters form by visual placement. For a learner, this captures something a node-and-edge graph misses: the embodied work of arranging, regrouping, and physically separating ideas as understanding develops. An examiner could interrogate a canvas by asking how groupings shifted over time, what was placed near what, what was moved to the periphery and later pulled back. Tldraw's "make real" feature, where rough sketches become functional interface elements, hints at how spatial work can carry intentional structure that AI can read.

Computational notebooks — Jupyter, Observable, Marimo, Quarto — interleave prose, code, and live output in a single document where the sequence is both narrative and executable. The notebook is unusual because the artefact is simultaneously the thinking and the result of the thinking. You can rerun any cell and see whether it still works, which is itself a form of evidence. For a learner, a notebook's history shows not just what was concluded but what was tried, which experiments produced unexpected results, and where the analytical path diverged from initial expectations.

Conversational interfaces that are not chat form a distinct category worth marking. Voice interaction removes the friction of typing without inheriting the chat-thread container problem, particularly when voice conversations are themselves anchored to substrate nodes. Structured Socratic dialogue, where the AI's role is to ask rather than answer, supports a different kind of cognitive work — one closer to good supervision than to information retrieval. Asynchronous anchored annotation, through tools like Hypothes.is, attaches conversation to specific passages in source material rather than floating it in a thread separated from what it is about. Each of these preserves the conversational affordance that chat gets right while addressing the substrate problem chat gets wrong.

Structured-input forms do pedagogical work through their constraints. Clinical documentation tools like SOAP notes force attention to subjective, objective, assessment, and plan in sequence; the structure itself teaches something about the shape of clinical reasoning. AI can sit behind such a form as a checker or prompt-generator, asking "your assessment doesn't follow from your objective findings — can you say more?" The interface looks nothing like chat or a graph, but it is AI-supported learning nonetheless, and the completed forms accumulate as a substrate of their own.

Code editors have become one of the more sophisticated examples of AI integration, and they are worth attending to precisely because they are not usually discussed as educational interfaces. Tools like Cursor and Claude Code ground the conversation with AI in specific files, lines, and project context; AI contributions appear as diffs that can be accepted, rejected, or modified. This is the spatial-anchoring move the original essay gestured at, implemented in a domain where it is widely accepted and undergoing rapid refinement. The substrate is the codebase with its git history; the interface is the editor with its AI sidebar; the traces are preserved in the commits. Learning to code in this environment produces exactly the kind of longitudinal record this essay is arguing for, as a matter of course rather than as a deliberate pedagogical choice.

Timeline interfaces make the temporal dimension primary. Roam Research's daily notes, chronological scrollback through a vault, commit log views of a project — these surface the rhythm of the work rather than the current state of the artefact. They suit domains where learning unfolds across long durations and where what matters is the trajectory rather than any single snapshot: clinical placements, longitudinal research projects, professional practice development over years.

None of these is the right answer in isolation; the right combination depends on the work being done. Many alternatives are already in production, being used by real people, and already preserving traces that chat discards. Across all of them, the substrate-preservation question — which choice of substrate, and whether it preserves the working out — is the design decision that determines what AI can later do with the record. The space of answers is much larger than the current default, and what remains to be asked is what can be done with the traces these substrates preserve.

## Interrogating the evidence of learning

A substrate that preserves traces is only useful if something can navigate the traces. Here the combination of a personal knowledge graph with AI access through a local MCP layer becomes genuinely new. The version-controlled vault is the worked example, and the question is what an examiner, supervisor, or indeed the learner themselves can do with it that no human working alone could do.

Diff-based interrogation is the most direct move. Show me the moments where this concept's note changed substantively. Show me where a section was deleted and never restored. Show me where an external source was first cited, and track how the framing of that source evolved across subsequent edits. This is evidence of position change rather than position held, which is a different thing from what any polished document can show. A note that started as one paragraph summarising an uncritical reading of a source and became, over six months, a more ambivalent engagement that pulls at the source's assumptions — that trajectory is the evidence of development, and it is preserved in the diffs in a way it is not preserved anywhere in the eventual polished output.

Link archaeology surfaces a different kind of evidence. When did this connection first appear? What was the learner reading or writing immediately before it formed? Which links have been added and removed multiple times, suggesting a relationship the learner has been wrestling with? A link that took six months to settle is different evidence from one made on first encounter with the concepts. The history of the link is itself a record of the learner's changing view of how two things relate.

Orphan and dead-end analysis reveals what the learner tried and chose not to pursue. Which notes were created and never linked to anything? Which were linked once and then abandoned? The traces of unfinished thinking are often more revealing than the polished connections, because they show what was attempted in good faith and discarded on reflection. An examiner could ask the learner to walk through a particular abandoned thread and explain why it was dropped. That conversation would be richer evidence of judgement than any defence of a finished argument, because it would require the learner to reconstruct an earlier state of their own thinking and account for the change.

Density and temporal rhythm expose the shape of the work. Where in the vault is the conceptual labour concentrated? Are there periods of intense linking followed by consolidation? Sustained engagement with a difficult idea looks different from skimming, and the rhythm of activity is itself a signature of how the learner works. A supervisor with access to this kind of view could notice when a student had stopped engaging with a problem and start a conversation about why, rather than waiting for the next scheduled meeting to find out.

Cross-referencing against external corpora is the move that does the most for the question of originality. AI can compare the learner's vault against the literature it cites, surfacing places where the learner's framing diverges from the source — sometimes through misunderstanding that a supervisor can correct, sometimes through genuine reinterpretation the learner can defend as deliberate. The interesting cases are the ones the learner can defend. This is what a test of original contribution might look like in practice, grounded in specific textual comparisons rather than the examiner's general sense of the field.

Comparison across cohorts surfaces individual contribution against a shared background. Where multiple learners independently arrive at similar connections, the shared structure of the field as enacted by learners becomes visible. Where one learner's vault contains links no peer has made, the individual's distinctive contribution becomes visible as something more than the examiner's hunch. Both views are useful, neither is currently possible without the substrate, and both can be generated by AI navigating the collection of vaults — with whatever privacy protections the institution and the learners agree on.

Grounded conversation is the interface through which these analyses become assessment. The examiner-learner conversation itself changes once it can draw on the process record. Instead of "tell me about your methodology chapter," it becomes "I notice you reframed your research question in week thirty-two — what happened?" Instead of "how does your framework differ from X's?" it becomes "here is a passage from X that your note on this concept engages with differently in the January version than in the March version — can you walk us through what shifted?" The questions become specific and harder to prepare for, because they emerge from the actual record rather than from a general template. This shifts the conversation from performance toward investigation.

The [[phd-becoming|companion piece on the PhD as becoming]] makes the full argument for what assessment on this substrate would look like. For this essay's purposes, what matters is that the substrate and the interrogation modes together constitute a different way of making judgements about learning — one grounded in traces rather than in proxies. The evidence of learning was always in the process. The argument here is that we now have infrastructure that can preserve the process and let us look at it.

## Where this sits in a larger architecture

This essay is one of three related pieces describing an alternative architecture for AI-supported professional education. The [[curriculum-infrastructure|curriculum infrastructure essay]] argues that institutional knowledge — curricula, competencies, prerequisite chains, accreditation evidence — belongs in a graph database with a typed schema, exposed through MCP to staff, regulators, and students. That is the right substrate for institutional structure because institutional structure is the kind of knowledge that benefits from being stable, typed, and queryable against a fixed ontology. The [[phd-becoming|PhD becoming piece]] argues that the thesis has become a failing proxy for the identity shift it was supposed to evidence, and that we can now look directly at the becoming rather than at its document-shaped shadow. This essay describes the substrate — the personal, version-controlled knowledge graph, exposed through a local tech stack — that makes both the individual learning story and the assessment of it possible.

The three arguments are complementary rather than competing. The institutional graph captures the curriculum as designed. The personal vault captures the learner's thinking as enacted. The assessment conversation sits at the intersection, drawing on both to ask specific questions about whether the designed curriculum has done the work it was meant to do for this particular learner's development. None of this requires abandoning existing tools. Learning management systems continue to host formal content. Libraries continue to hold the literature. What changes is that the learner's own environment — the substrate in which they think — becomes an explicit part of the architecture rather than an afterthought.

## Objections and limits

Several objections to this proposal deserve direct engagement rather than dismissal, and several others that initially appear substantial turn out on examination to apply with equal or greater force to the current arrangement.

The most serious concern is equity. A version-controlled personal knowledge graph exposed through a local MCP layer is not infrastructure everyone can set up and maintain. It requires technical fluency, time, institutional support, and in many cases hardware that not all learners have. This is true. It is also true of almost every dimension of doctoral and professional education as it currently exists. A student at a well-resourced institution has access to supervision, libraries, seminars, and peer networks that a part-time student juggling caring responsibilities at an under-resourced institution does not. Uneven distribution of good infrastructure is an argument for extending access to it, not for settling for the lowest common denominator. The alternative is to hold all learners to a lowest common denominator, which advantages no one and disadvantages the learners whose institutions are least able to compensate for what the common denominator lacks.

The concern about surveillance requires more unpacking than it is usually given. On the account developed here, the vault is the learner's — it lives locally, under their control, in formats they own. What they choose to expose to examiners or to an institution is an intentional decision. A PhD candidate who has kept a vault through their doctorate can choose to expose everything, expose nothing, or expose a curated subset relevant to particular assessment questions. They can produce specific views for specific purposes, in much the same way they currently produce a thesis as one particular artefact drawn from a much larger body of work. The difference is that the base material is richer and the views that can be generated from it are more varied; it is not that the learner loses control over what the institution sees. Framing this as surveillance assumes an all-or-nothing exposure that the architecture does not require and should not enforce. The institution does not get a key to the vault. The learner shows what they choose to show.

The worry about performing for the record is real, but the current alternative is no less performative. The thesis is a performance for the examiner. The viva is a performance for the panel. Portfolios, reflective writing, presentations, case reports — these are all performances. I prefer to think of them as observed behaviours from which examiners infer competence, which removes some of the pejorative weight of "performance" while keeping the underlying point that assessment has always involved learners producing things for evaluators to look at. The question is not whether learners will shape what they show, which they will and always have, but whether the substrate makes it possible to infer competence more accurately from a wider range of observed behaviours. A vault with a three-year diff history is harder to fabricate in service of a performance than a thesis is, because the performance would have to be sustained across years of commits rather than compiled in the months before submission. Grounded conversation in a viva is harder to prepare for than a defence of a polished document, because the questions can come from anywhere in the record. The risk of performing does not disappear; it becomes harder to do in ways that obscure actual development.

The concern about tacit knowledge applies with much greater force to the thesis than to the vault. A thesis captures the articulable result of a research process; the vault captures articulable intermediate states as well, including the moments where articulation was difficult and the learner wrestled with something they had not yet put into words. More importantly, AI navigating the vault can do what a good supervisor does with the tacit-knowledge problem: surface the places where something seems to be happening that the learner has not yet explained, and ask questions that help them explain it. The vault does not capture tacit knowledge, but it captures more of the trail leading toward articulation, and it enables a kind of interrogation that can help close the gap. No substrate fully solves the tacit knowledge problem; this one gets closer than any polished document.

The risk of substrate lock-in is real and worth naming. Markdown plus git is the most portable combination currently available, and the argument of this essay should be understood as an argument for portable formats rather than for any specific PKM tool. The current dominance of Obsidian in this space is a function of its particular design decisions being well-suited to many users; those decisions could be made differently and the substrate would still work as long as the files remained in open formats with a version history attached. Learners building vaults for multi-year use should be advised to treat the format as more important than the application.

Finally, there is the honest limit that the substrate captures what learners write, and only what learners write. Conversations that are never transcribed, thoughts that are never turned into notes, insights that happen in the shower and are never recorded — none of this makes it in. The vault is a partial record, not a complete one. This is a limit, but it is the same limit that applies to every form of educational assessment currently in use, with the added consideration that the vault captures more of the intermediate states than any alternative currently does.

## The essay as a node

I am aware of a rhetorical awkwardness that the original version of this essay carried and did not address. An argument that bounded linear documents fragment knowledge, presented in the form of a bounded linear document, leaves some work on the table. The rebuild does not fully resolve this — it remains an essay, and essays are linear by nature — but the infrastructure the argument depends on also contains the answer. This essay is a node in a vault. It is written in Obsidian, version-controlled in git, published through a GitHub Actions pipeline, and rendered by Quartz into a site where its connections to other notes and essays are visible as links and as a local graph around the node. A reader encountering the prose sees one view; a reader encountering the site sees another; a reader cloning the repository can see the commit history and watch the argument evolve across versions. The form of the argument and the substrate of the argument are the same thing, viewed through different interfaces. The awkwardness does not disappear, but naming it lets the essay sit more honestly within the architecture it describes.

## Traces and substrates

The specific substrates and interfaces I have described will be superseded. MCP is one protocol among what will probably become several; Obsidian is one PKM tool among what will probably become many more; the particular combination of git and GitHub Actions and Quartz that I use to publish this essay will be replaced by something better within a few years. The principle outlasts the details: learning leaves traces, and we should choose substrates and interfaces that preserve those traces in forms that AI can navigate and that learners can draw on to show what they have come to know and who they have become in the process of coming to know it.

The evidence of learning was never in the document. It was always in the process, and the process is now recoverable. The container schema has structured educational design for centuries; the chat interface has reproduced it in AI-mediated learning. Neither is adequate to the slow accumulation of judgement across years of interaction with a domain and the communities that work in it. The alternative is a commitment — to preserving the working out rather than only the worked result, and to building substrates and stacks that support that preservation across the long timescales on which real expertise develops.
