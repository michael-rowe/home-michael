It has been a couple of months since the last one, which was deliberate. I narrowed the summer down to finishing three things: a paper on AI and clinical reasoning with Euson Yeung, a chapter synopsis for a volume on AI and the future of health professions education, and the final edit of a book on AI for doctoral researchers with [[Benita Olivier]]. Everything else — this newsletter included — was set aside so those could close. They did, and it was the right trade.

The book is what I want to write about here, because finishing it left me with a question I have not been able to put down.

The book rests on one claim: developing the expertise to evaluate what AI produces requires the very skills that AI threatens to replace. That holds up. But it opens a harder question underneath it — **what do we actually add, once the machine can do everything we do, only better?** If the answer is "we check its work", that is not much of an answer, and it will not survive the next model release.

A few weeks ago Reid Hoffman published [a piece](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/) that lands very close to where Benita and I ended up, coming from a completely different direction. He had been talking to the artist Sougwen Chung, who trains robotic arms on a decade of their own drawing data and guides them live through an EEG sensor. Chung has gone further into machine legibility than almost anyone alive. And they draw a line: **machine readable, not machine executable.** Give the system everything it needs to know about how you work; don't give it enough that it takes you out of the loop.

What makes this more than a slogan is Chung's reason, which is practical rather than philosophical: *why would I want to build a system that takes away the thing I love the most from me?* They draw more now than they ever have.

Hoffman then says the thing I think is genuinely important, and it cuts against the standard advice in every AI adoption deck. *Automate the boring parts, keep the interesting parts* sounds obviously right, which is usually a warning sign. Tedium and skill formation are not opposites — they overlap constantly. Reps are boring, and reps are where pattern recognition, taste and judgement get built. The junior associate reading four hundred documents is bored, and is also building the instinct that later lets them spot what matters in a deal. Hand those reps over and something still gets built; it just accrues to whoever owns the model, trains on everyone's work rather than yours, and ships to your competitor in the same release.

I would add one thing to this, which is the piece I keep coming back to. The question is not really whether AI is good enough. It does not need to beat the best person in the room, or even the average one — it needs to beat whatever you would have done instead. The variable that actually decides whether that trade is safe is **whether an error-correction loop exists**: can a mistake be caught cheaply and fixed quickly? Where that loop is present, offloading is mostly fine. Where it is absent, you are not saving effort, you are accumulating unexamined error — and professional formation is precisely the setting where the loop is weakest, because the person who would catch the mistake is the person still being formed.

Which is why the strongest version of Chung's line is not a rule about tools at all. It is two questions to ask of any task: *Is this where my learning happens?* and *If the system does this instead of me, do I get worse?*

I would be interested to know where you have drawn that line in your own work, and whether you have drawn it deliberately or discovered it after the fact.

## One thing from me

I have spent most of my leave building **[Path](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/Posts/2026-07-18-introducing-path-cpd-portfolio-app)**, which went publicly available in July. It is a free tool for building a professional development portfolio: you pick a standard or framework you are working towards, capture evidence as you go, and it shows you where your case is strong and where the gaps are. That is the whole idea — the portfolio assembles itself out of work you were doing anyway, rather than being reconstructed in a panic the week before a submission deadline.

It is early, and honestly it is alpha. Access is invite-only for now, through a waiting list, because I would rather it be good for a few people than mediocre for many. A few people have been in touch about whether it might suit their members or their staff, which has been the most useful feedback I have had.

It is also, unavoidably, a working example of the argument above. Building it is where a lot of my own learning has happened this year, and I have been careful about which parts of that I hand over.

## Worth reading

Three pieces, all circling the same problem from different angles:

- Ke, Jin, Ong and colleagues: *[AI-induced never-skilling in medical education](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04438-y)* (Nature Medicine). The sharpest framing I have read this year. Their term is **never-skilling**, and they are careful to distinguish it from deskilling: deskilling is losing a capability you once had, never-skilling is failing to build it in the first place because AI was there during the formative years. For anyone who trains practitioners, that distinction changes what you are actually protecting.
- Reid Hoffman: *[Become machine-readable, not executable](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/)*. The piece above. Read it for the new-hire test if nothing else: for any task you are tempted to offload, ask whether you would be comfortable if someone joining your team never did it. If not, that task is load-bearing for expertise — including yours.
- Corbin, Bearman, Fawns and colleagues: *[Hybrid reading practices: how GenAI summarisers can support rather than replace reading](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075079.2026.2708138)* (Studies in Higher Education). The necessary counterweight, and the reason none of this is an argument for abstinence. Summarisers can genuinely support close reading — the difference is whether they are integrated deliberately or reached for by default.

## Quotes that resonated with me

> Every rep you take builds something that accrues to you. Every rep you hand over builds something too, but it accrues to whoever owns the model, gets trained on everyone's work rather than yours, and arrives in your competitor's hands in the same release. Those curves are not in the same race. The model's improvement is not your personal improvement.
>
> Reid Hoffman, *Become machine-readable, not executable*

> Why would I want to build a system that takes away the thing I love the most from me?
>
> Sougwen Chung, quoted in the same piece

> Our moral imagination is the raw material these systems learn from. That makes up how they will understand our world.
>
> Chloe Lubinski, Anthropic
