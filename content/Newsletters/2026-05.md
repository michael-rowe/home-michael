This issue is in a slightly different shape, and it's worth saying why. I've been uneasy with newsletters that are mostly a list of the author's own work — including this one. So from now on it will mostly be things I've found useful and think you might too: a couple of reads, some quotes, a debate worth following, and a single piece of my own, with a link to everything else.

A quick word on what I'm sitting with this month. Most of what I published circles one idea: the research harness — a way of setting out explicitly what an AI agent is *for* in a piece of doctoral work, and how it's allowed to operate. What I didn't expect is how much the idea has started to change my own practice. Over the past few weeks I've begun building a harness around my own scholarship and learning: a working setup that defines what work counts, what I let AI do, what stays my decision, and how it all gets recorded. It's still mostly scaffolding, but it's already changing how I choose what to work on from one day to the next. Has anyone else tried to make their own working rules with AI explicit, rather than leaving them ad hoc? I'd genuinely like to know what that looks like for you — just reply.

## Worth reading

Two pieces from this month, both squarely about how AI meets learning:

- **Keren, West & Desai — _[Promoting clinical expertise in the age of AI: no struggle, no mastery](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/10.1001/jama.2026.6097)_ (JAMA).** A clear argument that leaning on AI for the cognitively demanding parts of clinical work removes the productive struggle that expertise is built from. If you teach clinicians, it names a real tension.
- **Dickinson & Marshall — _[Trained to stop learning](https://wonkhe.com/blogs/trained-to-stop-learning-how-students-are-experiencing-assessment-and-learning-in-an-age-of-ai/)_ (Wonkhe).** A large UK survey (1,055 students) with a finding educators can actually act on: it is assessment *design*, not AI use itself, that decides whether AI ends up supporting or replacing learning.

## A debate worth following

A genuine disagreement played out this month over AI assessment scales — the traffic-light-style frameworks for declaring how much AI is permitted in a task. It's worth watching whichever way you lean.

**The critique.** Mark Bassett, in *[Blinded by the (traffic) lights](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/blinded-traffic-lights-intellectual-bankruptcy-ai-use-mark-a-bassett-0davc)*, argues the scales fail where it matters most: the middle bands let one piece of student work satisfy and violate adjacent levels at once; the question of when an assessment "begins" has no answer; and the AI tools themselves draw no line between what one level permits and the next forbids. His charge is that a label ends up communicating an institutional preference about conditions no institution actually controls.

**The reply.** Mike Perkins, a lead author of the AI Assessment Scale, agrees with more of this than you might expect — labelling a task and walking away is useless, detection doesn't work, and the first version leaned too hard on enforcement. But he argues v2 was deliberately reframed: the scale isn't an enforcement mechanism, it's a tool for *redesigning* assessment and communicating intent. His line worth holding onto, from *[On labels, scales, and what the AIAS actually claims to do](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/labels-scales-what-aias-actually-claims-do-dr-mike-perkins-gzhpe/)*: "a framework develops legitimately when its central claim becomes clearer under criticism."

Both are worth reading in full. Wherever you land, a real disagreement aired openly is more useful than either institutional silence or a false consensus.
