This one is late, and the reason is that I spent the summer finishing three things rather than starting new ones: a paper on AI and clinical reasoning with Euson Yeung, a chapter synopsis for a volume on AI and the future of health professions education, and the final edit of the book on AI for doctoral researchers I've been writing with [[Benita Olivier]]. All three are done. It was a good trade, and I'd make it again, though it did mean everything else went quiet for a while.

The book is what I want to write about, because finishing it left me stuck on something. Its central claim is that you need real expertise to judge what AI produces, and that this expertise gets built by doing the work AI is offering to take off your hands. I still think that's right. What it doesn't settle is the question sitting underneath it, which is what we are actually contributing once the machine does the work better than we do. If the answer is that we check its output, that won't survive many more model releases.

Reid Hoffman published [a piece](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/) a couple of weeks ago that arrives somewhere very close to where Benita and I ended up, having come at it from a completely different direction. He'd been talking to Sougwen Chung, an artist who trains robotic arms on a decade of her own drawing data and then guides them live through an EEG sensor while she works. Chung has made herself more machine-legible than almost anyone alive, and she still draws a line, which she describes as wanting to be machine readable but not machine executable. The system can know everything about how she works. It cannot have enough to do the work without her.

Her reason for the line is worth sitting with, because it isn't the one you'd expect. She doesn't argue that the machine would produce worse drawings. She asks why she would want to build something that takes away the thing she loves most, and notes that she draws more now than she ever has.

What Hoffman does with this is the part I keep returning to. The standard advice everywhere is to automate the tedious parts of your work and keep the interesting parts, and he points out that tedium and skill formation overlap far more than that advice allows. Reps are boring, and reps are also where pattern recognition and judgement come from. His example is the junior associate reading four hundred documents, who is bored, and who is at the same time building the instinct that later lets her see what matters in a deal nobody else has read properly. Hand that reading to a machine and the instinct still forms somewhere. It just forms in the model rather than in her, and the model belongs to someone else.

I'd add one thing, which is about where the line actually falls in practice. The useful question is not whether AI is good enough in some absolute sense. It only has to be better than whatever you would have done instead, which is a much lower bar than people assume and explains why so much AI use is perfectly sensible. What decides whether the trade is safe is whether there is an error-correction loop around the task: some reliable way for a mistake to surface and get fixed while it still costs little. Where that loop exists, handing work over is mostly fine, because the errors get caught. Where it doesn't, you aren't saving effort so much as accumulating unexamined error, and you may not find out for years.

Professional formation is the setting where the loop is weakest, since the person who would notice the mistake is the person still being formed. That's the case I care about, and it's why I think this is more than a productivity question for anyone who trains practitioners.

Chung's line turns out to be two questions you can ask of any task. Is this where my learning happens, and if the system does this instead of me, do I get worse? I'd be interested to know where you've drawn that line in your own work, and whether you drew it on purpose or noticed afterwards that you'd drawn it.

## One thing from me

Most of my leave went on **[Path](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/Posts/2026-07-18-introducing-path-cpd-portfolio-app)**, which became publicly available in July. It's a free tool for putting a professional development portfolio together. You choose a standard or framework you're working towards, capture evidence as you go, and it shows you where your case is already strong and where the gaps are, so that the portfolio accumulates out of work you were doing anyway instead of being assembled in a panic the week before a deadline.

It's alpha, and access is invite-only through a waiting list for the moment, on the grounds that I'd rather it worked properly for a few people first. A few people have got in touch to ask whether it might suit their members or their staff, which has been the most useful feedback I've had.

It's also, unavoidably, a working instance of everything above. Building it is where a good deal of my own learning has happened this year, and I've thought carefully about which parts of it I hand over.

Everything else from the past couple of months is on the [Recently added](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/recently-added) page.

## Worth reading

Three pieces circling the same problem from different angles:

- Ke, Jin, Ong and colleagues: *[AI-induced never-skilling in medical education](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04438-y)* (Nature Medicine). The sharpest framing I've read this year. They separate never-skilling from deskilling, which sounds like a small distinction and isn't. Deskilling means losing a capability you once had; never-skilling means the capability never forms, because AI was there through the years when it would have been built. If you train practitioners, that changes what you're protecting and when you have to protect it.
- Reid Hoffman: *[Become machine-readable, not executable](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/)*. The piece above, worth reading for the new-hire test if nothing else. For any task you're thinking of offloading, ask whether you'd be comfortable if someone joining your team never did it. If you wouldn't, the task is load-bearing for expertise, including your own.
- Corbin, Bearman, Fawns and colleagues: *[Hybrid reading practices: how GenAI summarisers can support rather than replace reading](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075079.2026.2708138)* (Studies in Higher Education). The counterweight, and the reason none of this amounts to an argument for abstinence. Summarisers can genuinely support close reading, and the difference lies in whether they're built into a considered practice or reached for by default.

## Quotes that resonated with me

> Every rep you take builds something that accrues to you. Every rep you hand over builds something too, but it accrues to whoever owns the model, gets trained on everyone's work rather than yours, and arrives in your competitor's hands in the same release. Those curves are not in the same race. The model's improvement is not your personal improvement.
>
> Reid Hoffman, *Become machine-readable, not executable*

> Why would I want to build a system that takes away the thing I love the most from me?
>
> Sougwen Chung, quoted in the same piece

> Our moral imagination is the raw material these systems learn from. That makes up how they will understand our world.
>
> Chloe Lubinski, Anthropic
