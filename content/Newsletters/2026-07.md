What do we add, once the machine can do everything we do, only better?

If the answer is that we check its work, that isn't much of an answer. It won't survive the next model release.

I've spent most of the summer on that question, which is most of why this newsletter went quiet. I narrowed things down to finishing three pieces of work: a paper on AI and clinical reasoning with Euson Yeung, a chapter synopsis for a volume on AI and the future of health professions education, and the final edit of a book on AI for doctoral researchers with [[Benita Olivier]]. They're done, and it was the right trade.

The book rests on one claim: developing the expertise to evaluate what AI produces requires the very skills that AI threatens to replace. That holds up. It's also what opens the question above, and I haven't been able to put it down since.

A few weeks ago Reid Hoffman published [a piece](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/) that lands very close to where Benita and I ended up, coming from somewhere else entirely. He'd been talking to the artist Sougwen Chung, who trains robotic arms on a decade of their own drawing data and guides them live through an EEG sensor. Chung has gone further into machine legibility than almost anyone alive. And they draw a line: machine readable, not machine executable. Give the system everything it needs to know about how you work. Don't give it enough that it takes you out of the loop.

Chung's reason is practical rather than philosophical. *Why would I want to build a system that takes away the thing I love the most from me?* They draw more now than they ever have.

What follows from that cuts against the advice in every AI adoption deck. *Automate the boring parts, keep the interesting parts* sounds obviously right, which is usually a warning sign. Tedium and skill formation aren't opposites. They overlap constantly. Reps are boring, and reps are where pattern recognition, taste and judgement get built. The junior associate reading four hundred documents is bored, and is also building the instinct that later lets them spot what matters in a deal. Hand those reps over and something still gets built. It just doesn't get built in you, and it doesn't stay yours.

One thing I'd add. The question isn't really whether AI is good enough. It doesn't need to beat the best person in the room, or even the average one. It needs to beat whatever you'd have done instead. What decides whether that trade is safe is whether an error-correction loop exists: can a mistake be caught cheaply, and fixed quickly? Where the loop is there, offloading is mostly fine. Where it's missing, you're not saving effort, you're accumulating error that nobody has looked at.

Professional formation is where that loop is weakest, because the person who would catch the mistake is the person still being formed.

So the useful version of Chung's line isn't a rule about tools. It's two questions to ask of any task. Is this where my learning happens? If the system does this instead of me, do I get worse?

I'd be interested to know where you've drawn that line in your own work, and whether you drew it deliberately or found it after the fact.

## One thing from me

I've spent most of my leave building **[Path](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/Posts/2026-07-18-introducing-path-cpd-portfolio-app)**, which became publicly available in July. It's a free tool for building a professional development portfolio. You pick a standard or framework you're working towards, capture evidence as you go, and it shows you where your case is strong and where the gaps are. The portfolio assembles itself out of work you were doing anyway, instead of being reconstructed in a panic the week before a submission deadline.

It's early, and it's alpha. Access is invite-only through a waiting list for now, because I'd rather it were good for a few people than mediocre for many. A few people have been in touch about whether it might suit their members or their staff, which has been the most useful feedback I've had.

It's also, unavoidably, an instance of the argument above. Building it is where a lot of my own learning has happened this year, and I've been careful about which parts of that I hand over.

Everything else from the past two months is on the [Recently added](https://michael-rowe.github.io/home-michael/recently-added) page.

## Worth reading

Three pieces, circling the same problem from different angles:

- Ke, Jin, Ong and colleagues: *[AI-induced never-skilling in medical education](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04438-y)* (Nature Medicine). The sharpest framing I've read this year. Their term is **never-skilling**, and they're careful to separate it from deskilling. Deskilling is losing a capability you once had. Never-skilling is failing to build it at all, because AI was there through the formative years. If you train practitioners, that distinction changes what you're actually protecting.
- Reid Hoffman: *[Become machine-readable, not executable](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/become-machine-readable-executable-reid-hoffman-h3j2c/)*. The piece above. Read it for the new-hire test if nothing else: for any task you're tempted to offload, ask whether you'd be comfortable if someone joining your team never did it. If not, that task is load-bearing for expertise, including yours.
- Corbin, Bearman, Fawns and colleagues: *[Hybrid reading practices: how GenAI summarisers can support rather than replace reading](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03075079.2026.2708138)* (Studies in Higher Education). The necessary counterweight, and the reason none of this is an argument for abstinence. Summarisers can genuinely support close reading. What matters is whether they're integrated deliberately or reached for by default.

## Quotes that resonated with me

> Every rep you take builds something that accrues to you. Every rep you hand over builds something too, but it accrues to whoever owns the model, gets trained on everyone's work rather than yours, and arrives in your competitor's hands in the same release. Those curves are not in the same race. The model's improvement is not your personal improvement.
>
> Reid Hoffman, *Become machine-readable, not executable*

> Why would I want to build a system that takes away the thing I love the most from me?
>
> Sougwen Chung, quoted in the same piece

> Our moral imagination is the raw material these systems learn from. That makes up how they will understand our world.
>
> Chloe Lubinski, Anthropic
